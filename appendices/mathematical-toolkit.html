Appendix: Mathematical toolkit

The course repeatedly moves between kinetic, fluid, and field descriptions. This appendix collects the energy-weighted second moment of the kinetic equation and the differential operators used in Cartesian, cylindrical, and spherical coordinates, with collapsed derivations for reconstructing each result.

Energy-weighted second moment

How does the kinetic equation produce an energy equation? Weighting by the single-particle kinetic energy and integrating over velocity converts velocity-space transport into electromagnetic work, spatial transport, and collisional energy exchange. The result is the bridge between a distribution function and the fluid energy equations used later in the script.

Learning objectives

  • define the bulk, internal, and heat-flux contributions to a species energy moment
  • carry out the velocity-space integration by parts without dropping the boundary condition
  • identify why the magnetic part of the Lorentz force does no work
  • separate the total-energy equation from the internal-energy equation
Unit ledger The species distribution is defined by 𝑑𝑁𝑠=𝑓𝑠d3𝒓d3𝒗. The energy density and energy flux below are in Jm−3 and Wm−2, respectively.

The three terms in the energy flux have distinct meanings: advection of total particle kinetic energy 𝑊𝑠𝒖𝑠 (bulk plus internal), pressure work transport 𝑷𝑠⋅𝒖𝑠, and random-energy transport 𝒒𝑠. They must not be merged into a scalar heat flux unless isotropy and a closure have been stated.

Derivation: total energy from the kinetic equation

Step: Weight the kinetic equation

Start from the non-conservative form

𝜕 𝑓 𝑠 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝒗 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝑓 𝑠 ) + 𝒂 𝑠 ⋅ 𝜕 𝑓 𝑠 𝜕 𝒗 = 𝐶 𝑠 [ 𝑓 ] .

Since 𝒗 is independent of 𝒓, the streaming term is also 𝛁·(𝑓𝑠𝒗). Define the single-particle kinetic energy

𝜀 𝑠 ( 𝒗 ) = 𝑚 𝑠 | 𝒗 | 2 2 .

Multiply the kinetic equation by this energy and integrate over all velocity space:

∫ 𝜀 𝑠 𝜕 𝑓 𝑠 𝜕 𝑡 d 3 𝒗 + ∫ 𝜀 𝑠 𝛁 · ( 𝑓 𝑠 𝒗 ) d 3 𝒗 + ∫ 𝜀 𝑠 𝒂 𝑠 ⋅ 𝜕 𝑓 𝑠 𝜕 𝒗 d 3 𝒗 = ∫ 𝜀 𝑠 𝐶 𝑠 [ 𝑓 ] d 3 𝒗 .

Step: Evaluate the temporal and streaming terms

The temporal term is

∫ 𝜀 𝑠 𝜕 𝑓 𝑠 𝜕 𝑡 d 3 𝒗 = 𝜕 ∫ 𝜀 𝑠 𝑓 𝑠 d 3 𝒗 𝜕 𝑡 = 𝜕 𝑊 𝑠 𝜕 𝑡 .

The streaming term commutes with the velocity integral:

∫ 𝜀 𝑠 𝛁 · ( 𝑓 𝑠 𝒗 ) d 3 𝒗 = 𝛁 · ( 𝑚 𝑠 ∫ | 𝒗 | 2 𝒗 𝑓 𝑠 d 3 𝒗 2 ) .

Step: Integrate the acceleration term by parts

Write the contracted velocity divergence explicitly as

𝛁 · 𝒗 ( 𝑨 ) = ∑ 𝑗 𝜕 𝐴 𝑗 𝜕 𝑣 𝑗 .

The velocity-space product rule is

𝜀 𝑠 𝒂 𝑠 ⋅ 𝜕 𝑓 𝑠 𝜕 𝒗 = 𝛁 · 𝒗 ( 𝜀 𝑠 𝑓 𝑠 𝒂 𝑠 ) − 𝑓 𝑠 𝒂 𝑠 ⋅ 𝜕 𝜀 𝑠 𝜕 𝒗 − 𝜀 𝑠 𝑓 𝑠 𝛁 · 𝒗 ( 𝒂 𝑠 ) .

For the Lorentz acceleration, 𝛁·𝒗(𝒂𝑠)=0: the electric field is independent of velocity and the magnetic linear map is antisymmetric, hence has zero trace. The last term therefore vanishes.

The first term becomes the surface integral

∫ boundary V_v 𝜀 𝑠 𝑓 𝑠 𝒂 𝑠 ⋅ 𝑑 𝑺 𝑣 ,

which vanishes at infinite speed by the boundary assumption. Because 𝜕𝜀𝑠/𝜕𝒗=𝑚𝑠𝒗, the remaining term is

− 𝑚 𝑠 ∫ 𝑓 𝑠 𝒂 𝑠 ⋅ 𝒗 d 3 𝒗 .

Insert the Lorentz acceleration. The magnetic contribution vanishes pointwise, leaving

− 𝑞 𝑠 𝑬 ⋅ ∫ 𝒗 𝑓 𝑠 d 3 𝒗 = − 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝑬 ⋅ 𝒖 𝑠 .

Define the collision power

𝑄 𝑠 = ∫ 𝜀 𝑠 𝐶 𝑠 [ 𝑓 ] d 3 𝒗 .

Moving the electric term to the right gives the conservative energy equation

𝜕 𝑊 𝑠 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝛁 · ( 𝑚 𝑠 ∫ | 𝒗 | 2 𝒗 𝑓 𝑠 d 3 𝒗 2 ) = 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝑬 ⋅ 𝒖 𝑠 + 𝑄 𝑠 .

Step: Decompose the energy flux

Substitute

| 𝒗 | 2 = | 𝒖 𝑠 | 2 + 2 𝒖 𝑠 ⋅ 𝒄 𝑠 + | 𝒄 𝑠 | 2 .

∫ 𝒄 𝑠 𝑓 𝑠 d 3 𝒗 = 𝟎 .

The surviving flux terms are

𝑚 𝑠 ∫ | 𝒗 | 2 𝒗 𝑓 𝑠 d 3 𝒗 2 = 𝐾 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 + 𝑈 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 + 𝑷 𝑠 ⋅ 𝒖 𝑠 + 𝒒 𝑠 .

Substitution gives the stated total-energy equation.

Derivation: subtract bulk energy and identify pressure work

Step: Start from the momentum balance

The first moment of the kinetic equation is

𝜕 𝜌 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝛁 · ( 𝜌 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 + 𝑷 𝑠 ) = 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 ( 𝑬 + 𝒖 𝑠 × 𝑩 ) + 𝑹 𝑠 .

Dot this equation with 𝒖𝑠. The number-conserving assumption gives 𝜕𝜌𝑠/𝜕𝑡+𝛁·(𝜌𝑠𝒖𝑠)=0, so the product rule yields

𝜕 𝐾 𝑠 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝛁 · ( 𝐾 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 ) = 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝑬 ⋅ 𝒖 𝑠 − 𝒖 𝑠 ⋅ 𝛁 · ( 𝑷 𝑠 ) + 𝒖 𝑠 ⋅ 𝑹 𝑠 .

Step: Subtract bulk energy from total energy

The total-energy equation contains the same electromagnetic power. Insert 𝑊𝑠=𝐾𝑠+𝑈𝑠 and subtract the bulk identity:

𝜕 𝑈 𝑠 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝛁 · ( 𝑈 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 + 𝑷 𝑠 ⋅ 𝒖 𝑠 + 𝒒 𝑠 ) = 𝒖 𝑠 ⋅ 𝛁 · ( 𝑷 𝑠 ) + 𝑄 𝑠 − 𝒖 𝑠 ⋅ 𝑹 𝑠 .

Step: Rewrite pressure work

Apply

𝛁 · ( 𝑷 𝑠 ⋅ 𝒖 𝑠 ) = 𝒖 𝑠 ⋅ 𝛁 · ( 𝑷 𝑠 ) + 𝑷 𝑠 : 𝛁 ( 𝒖 𝑠 ) .

The first term cancels, leaving the internal-energy equation with pressure work and heat-flux transport. For 𝑷𝑠=𝑝𝑠𝑰,

𝑷 𝑠 : 𝛁 ( 𝒖 𝑠 ) = 𝑝 𝑠 𝛁 · ( 𝒖 𝑠 ) .

This is the isotropic limit.

Knowledge check

  1. Why does the magnetic part of the Lorentz force not appear in the single-species energy source?
    Answer Its power is proportional to 𝒗 ⋅ ( 𝒗 × 𝑩 ) , which is zero for every particle. The electric part contributes 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝑬 ⋅ 𝒖 𝑠 in W m − 3 .
  2. Which term transports random kinetic energy relative to the species flow?
    Answer

    The heat-flux vector

    𝒒 𝑠 = 𝑚 𝑠 ∫ | 𝒄 𝑠 | 2 𝒄 𝑠 𝑓 𝑠 d 3 𝒗 2

    transports random energy. It is a third central velocity moment and needs a closure in a truncated fluid model.

  3. What assumption removes the velocity-space surface term in the energy derivation?
    Answer The distribution and velocity-space flux must decay fast enough that the surface integral at | 𝒗 | → ∞ vanishes. If a high-energy boundary or source is present, that flux must be retained.
  4. How does isotropic pressure simplify the internal-energy equation?
    Answer With 𝑷 𝑠 = 𝑝 𝑠 𝑰 , the tensor contraction becomes 𝑷 𝑠 : 𝛁 ( 𝒖 𝑠 ) = 𝑝 𝑠 𝛁 · ( 𝒖 𝑠 ) and the internal energy is 𝑈 𝑠 = 3 𝑝 𝑠 / 2 in three dimensions.

Vector operators and coordinate geometry

Plasma equations are often simplest in the coordinates selected by the geometry: Cartesian coordinates for a local wave, cylindrical coordinates for a column or pinch, and spherical coordinates for a nearly isotropic expansion. The physical vector is unchanged by the coordinate choice, but the basis vectors and area elements vary. Where do the extra factors of 𝑟 and sin(𝜃) come from? They are metric scale factors, not new physics.

Learning objectives

  • state the gradient, divergence, curl, and scalar Laplacian in orthogonal coordinates
  • specialize the formulas to Cartesian, cylindrical, and spherical systems
  • use a divergence or curl identity as a dimensional and limiting-case check
  • choose coordinates that expose the symmetry of a plasma configuration
Unit ledger Coordinates 𝑥,𝑦,𝑧,𝑟 are lengths in m, angles 𝜑 and 𝜃 are dimensionless, and a scalar field 𝜓 and vector field 𝑨 retain whatever physical units the model assigns. Thus 𝛁(𝜓) has units of 𝜓 per m, 𝛁·(𝑨) has units of 𝑨 per m, and 𝛁×(𝑨) has the same units. The formulas below are dimensional coordinate identities; no normalized radius is implied.

  1. Why does the cylindrical divergence contain 1 / 𝑟 ?

    𝜕 𝑟 𝐴 𝑟 𝜕 𝑟 𝑟 ,

  2. What does the factor 𝑟 sin ( 𝜃 ) represent in spherical coordinates?
    Answer It is the physical line scale for an azimuthal angle increment: 𝑑 𝑙 𝜑 = 𝑟 sin ( 𝜃 ) 𝑑 𝜑 . It also enters the face areas and therefore the divergence, curl, and Laplacian.
  3. Which coordinate system is usually the cleanest starting point for a locally plane plasma wave?
