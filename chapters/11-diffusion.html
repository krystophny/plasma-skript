11. Plasma diffusion

Diffusion is the macroscopic signature of many small changes in particle trajectories. Collisions randomize particle directions. Across a density gradient, more particles arrive from the dense side than from the dilute side, producing a net flux toward lower density even when individual steps are symmetric. Magnetic fields do not remove diffusion, but they make the transport direction dependent. This transport hierarchy follows the standard collisional and magnetized plasma description in [12].

11.1 Random walks and the diffusion coefficient

A symmetric random walk has no preferred direction, so its mean displacement remains zero. Its variance does not remain zero: independent steps add in quadrature. That distinction is the origin of a diffusion equation.

Learning objectives

  • derive the diffusion coefficient from a symmetric random walk
  • connect Fick’s law to the continuity equation
  • interpret the diffusive timescale and the Gaussian spreading solution
  • state which assumptions distinguish diffusion from directed advection

Unit ledger A diffusion coefficient 𝐷 is in m2s−1, and particle flux 𝚪 is in m−2s−1. The normalized variables 𝜉=𝑥/𝐿0 and 𝜏=𝑡/𝜏0 are dimensionless. In the one-dimensional Green function,

𝑁 0 = ∫ − ∞ ∞ 𝑛 ( 𝑥 , 𝑡 ) d 𝑥

is the conserved column density in m−2.

Angle brackets denote an ensemble average over possible random walks. A finite collection of walkers need not have exactly zero mean at each time. The ensemble variance describes spreading around the starting point, not a directed displacement of the whole cloud.

Derivation: from step statistics to the diffusion equation

Step: Compute the moments of one random step

Let one step be +Δ𝑥 or −Δ𝑥 with equal probability. Then

⟨ Δ 𝑥 ⟩ = Δ 𝑥 2 + − Δ 𝑥 2 = 0 , ⟨ ( Δ 𝑥 ) 2 ⟩ = ( Δ 𝑥 ) 2 2 + ( − Δ 𝑥 ) 2 2 = ( Δ 𝑥 ) 2 .

Step: Accumulate independent steps

After 𝑁=𝑡/(Δ𝑡) statistically independent steps, cross terms in the squared displacement vanish because the individual means are zero. Thus

⟨ 𝑥 ⟩ = 0 , ⟨ 𝑥 2 ⟩ = 𝑁 ( Δ 𝑥 ) 2 = ( ( Δ 𝑥 ) 2 𝑡 Δ 𝑡 ) = 2 𝐷 𝑡 .

This defines the one-dimensional coefficient 𝐷=(Δ𝑥)2/(2Δ𝑡).

Step: Derive the local diffusive flux

Consider a cell of width Δ𝑥. Right-moving and left-moving populations sample densities displaced by one step. Expanding those densities to first order gives

𝚪 𝐷 = − ( ( Δ 𝑥 ) 2 2 Δ 𝑡 ) 𝛁 ( 𝑛 ) = − 𝐷 𝛁 ( 𝑛 ) .

Particle conservation is

𝜕 𝑛 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝛁 · ( 𝚪 ) = 0 .

Step: Obtain the diffusion equation and Green function

Substitution yields

𝜕 𝑛 𝜕 𝑡 = 𝛁 · ( 𝐷 𝛁 ( 𝑛 ) ) .

For uniform 𝐷, this reduces to 𝜕𝑛/𝜕𝑡=𝐷∇2(𝑛). A point-like initial sheet 𝑛(𝑥,0)=𝑁0𝛿(𝑥) has Green-function solution

𝑛 ( 𝑥 , 𝑡 ) = 𝑁 0 4 𝜋 𝐷 𝑡 exp ( − 𝑥 2 4 𝐷 𝑡 ) .

Its second moment is ⟨𝑥2⟩=2𝐷𝑡. Independent Cartesian directions add, giving ⟨|𝒓|2⟩=6𝐷𝑡 in three dimensions.

Knowledge check

  1. Why does a symmetric random walk have zero mean displacement but nonzero spreading?
    Answer The positive and negative steps cancel in the first moment, but the squared steps are positive and add. Thus ⟨ 𝑥 ⟩ = 0 while ⟨ 𝑥 2 ⟩ grows linearly with time.
  2. What physical statement is encoded by Fick’s law?
    Answer The diffusive particle flux points down the density gradient: 𝚪 𝐷 = − 𝐷 𝛁 ( 𝑛 ) . The coefficient 𝐷 measures the strength of that response.
  3. How does the characteristic diffusion time scale with system size?
    Answer For a fixed coefficient, 𝜏 D = 𝐿 2 / 𝐷 , so doubling the length makes the diffusive time four times larger.
  4. Which assumption separates diffusion from ballistic transport?
    Answer Diffusion requires many weakly correlated steps and a local gradient over the observation time. If a particle retains its direction over the whole experiment, a ballistic description is more appropriate.

11.2 Diffusion in weakly ionized plasmas

In a weakly ionized plasma, collisions with neutrals provide the drag that converts a force or a pressure gradient into a drift. The same momentum equation therefore contains both mobility and diffusion.

Learning objectives

  • reduce the collisional momentum equation to a drift-diffusion law
  • define signed mobility and positive diffusion coefficients
  • use the Einstein relation without losing the charge-sign convention
  • identify when the weakly ionized approximation is local and isothermal
Unit ledger The mobility 𝜇𝑠 has units of velocity divided by electric field, m2V−1s−1, and 𝐷𝑠 is in m2s−1. This electric-field mobility includes charge, unlike the force-based mobility introduced in Chapter 10.
Derivation: collisional force balance

Step: Apply the isothermal pressure law

Start with the species momentum equation and use

𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑠 ) = 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 𝛁 ( 𝑛 𝑠 ) .

Step: Take the long-time force balance

On times longer than 𝜈𝑠−1, neglect inertia and solve

𝑚 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝜈 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 = 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝑬 − 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 𝛁 ( 𝑛 𝑠 ) .

Division by 𝑚𝑠𝑛𝑠𝜈𝑠 gives

𝒖 𝑠 = 𝑞 𝑠 𝑚 𝑠 𝜈 𝑠 𝑬 − 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 𝑚 𝑠 𝜈 𝑠 ( 𝛁 ( 𝑛 𝑠 ) 𝑛 𝑠 ) .

Step: Identify mobility and diffusion

Define the signed mobility and diffusion coefficient

𝜇 𝑠 𝑞 = 𝑞 𝑠 𝑚 𝑠 𝜈 𝑠 , 𝐷 𝑠 = 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 𝑚 𝑠 𝜈 𝑠 .

Multiplication by 𝑛𝑠 produces the particle flux. With the positive mobility magnitude |𝜇𝑠𝑞|=|𝑞𝑠|/(𝑚𝑠𝜈𝑠), the Einstein relation is

𝐷 𝑠 | 𝜇 𝑠 𝑞 | = 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 | 𝑞 𝑠 | .

Step: State the approximation boundary

The derivation assumes local, isothermal, unmagnetized drag. Inertia matters when the forcing varies on a time comparable to 𝜈𝑠−1, and a scalar coefficient is insufficient when a magnetic field makes the response tensorial.

Knowledge check

  1. Which term in the weakly ionized flux is driven by a density gradient?
    Answer The Fick term − 𝐷 𝑠 𝛁 ( 𝑛 𝑠 ) is gradient driven. It points down the density gradient and is distinct from the electric-force term.
  2. How does increasing the neutral collision frequency affect unmagnetized diffusion?
    Answer At fixed temperature, 𝐷 𝑠 = 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 / ( 𝑚 𝑠 𝜈 𝑠 ) decreases inversely with 𝜈 𝑠 because the mean free path and drift response are both shortened.
  3. Why must the charge sign be kept separate from the positive mobility magnitude?
    Answer Electrons and ions drift in opposite directions under the same electric field. With 𝜇 𝑠 = | 𝑞 𝑠 | / ( 𝑚 𝑠 𝜈 𝑠 ) , the electric drift is + 𝜇 𝑠 𝑬 for positive ions and − 𝜇 𝑠 𝑬 for electrons. The charge magnitude is already included in 𝜇 𝑠 .
  4. When is the scalar Einstein relation insufficient?
    Answer It is insufficient when the response is anisotropic, such as in a magnetic field, or when the plasma is non-isothermal or strongly time-dependent.

11.3 Ambipolar diffusion and the ambipolar electric field

Electrons usually diffuse faster than ions because their mass is smaller. The resulting charge separation creates an electric field that couples their transport. With zero-current boundary conditions in the transport direction, the field adjusts until the species carry the same particle flux. This coupled transport is ambipolar diffusion.

Learning objectives

  • write the electron and ion fluxes with explicit charge signs
  • derive the ambipolar electric field from equal particle fluxes
  • obtain the ambipolar diffusion coefficient
  • explain the role of quasi-neutrality and zero net current
Unit ledger The ambipolar diffusion coefficient 𝐷𝑎 is in m2s−1.

Charge conservation gives 𝜕𝜌𝑞/𝜕𝑡+𝛁·(𝒋)=0. Maintaining quasi-neutrality therefore constrains the leading current divergence; it does not require 𝒋=𝟎. In one-dimensional steady transport, current is spatially constant, and a zero-current boundary sets that constant to zero. This additional condition gives the equal fluxes used below.

Derivation: quasi-neutral flux balance

Step: Write the two species fluxes

For positive ions, the electric force drives a flux in the direction of 𝑬:

𝚪 𝑖 = 𝜇 𝑖 𝑛 𝑬 − 𝐷 𝑖 𝛁 ( 𝑛 ) .

Electrons have the opposite charge:

𝚪 𝑒 = − 𝜇 𝑒 𝑛 𝑬 − 𝐷 𝑒 𝛁 ( 𝑛 ) .

Step: Impose zero current

The current-free condition is

𝟎 = 𝒋 = 𝑒 ( 𝚪 𝑖 − 𝚪 𝑒 ) .

Equating the two fluxes and collecting the field terms gives

( 𝜇 𝑖 + 𝜇 𝑒 ) 𝑛 𝑬 = ( 𝐷 𝑖 − 𝐷 𝑒 ) 𝛁 ( 𝑛 ) .

Hence

𝑬 𝑎 = ( 𝐷 𝑖 − 𝐷 𝑒 𝜇 𝑖 + 𝜇 𝑒 ) ( 𝛁 ( 𝑛 ) 𝑛 ) .

Step: Insert the ambipolar field into the flux

The ion flux becomes

𝚪 𝑖 = ( 𝜇 𝑖 𝑛 ( 𝐷 𝑖 − 𝐷 𝑒 ) 𝜇 𝑖 + 𝜇 𝑒 ) ( 𝛁 ( 𝑛 ) 𝑛 ) − 𝐷 𝑖 𝛁 ( 𝑛 ) .

Putting both terms over the common denominator produces

𝚪 𝑖 = − 𝜇 𝑖 𝐷 𝑒 + 𝜇 𝑒 𝐷 𝑖 𝜇 𝑖 + 𝜇 𝑒 𝛁 ( 𝑛 ) .

The same result follows from the electron flux, so define

𝐷 𝑎 = 𝜇 𝑖 𝐷 𝑒 + 𝜇 𝑒 𝐷 𝑖 𝜇 𝑖 + 𝜇 𝑒 .

Step: Interpret the mobility hierarchy

For equal temperatures and comparable collision models, often 𝜇𝑒≫𝜇𝑖. Then

𝐷 𝑎 ≈ 𝐷 𝑖 + ( 𝜇 𝑖 𝜇 𝑒 ) 𝐷 𝑒 .

Electron diffusion is consequently largely converted into the ambipolar electric field rather than a net current.

Knowledge check

  1. Why does an ambipolar electric field form?
    Answer The more mobile species would otherwise diffuse away faster, creating charge separation. The resulting field opposes that separation until the electron and ion particle fluxes match.
  2. What condition was used to derive the ambipolar coefficient?
    Answer For singly charged hydrogen we imposed zero net current, 𝒋 = 𝑒 ( 𝚪 𝑖 − 𝚪 𝑒 ) = 𝟎 , together with quasi-neutral density.
  3. Can ambipolar diffusion carry particles when the current vanishes?
    Answer Yes. Equal oppositely charged particle fluxes cancel in the current but add to the total particle transport.
  4. What changes if the plasma is not quasi-neutral on the transport scale?
    Answer The electric field must then be obtained from Poisson’s equation and a charge-separation model; the equal-flux ambipolar closure is no longer sufficient.

11.4 Cross-field diffusion in a magnetic field

Along a magnetic field, a colliding particle can random-walk as if it were unmagnetized. Across the field, the guiding center is tied to a field line between collisions. A collision interrupts gyromotion and creates the cross-field step.

Learning objectives

  • derive the parallel and perpendicular diffusion coefficients
  • identify the magnetization parameter |Ω𝑠|/𝜈𝑠
  • separate ordinary diffusion from Hall-like transverse response
  • explain why the collisionless limit suppresses classical cross-field diffusion
Unit ledger The magnetization parameter |Ω𝑠|/𝜈𝑠 is dimensionless. Parallel, perpendicular, and Hall diffusion coefficients are in m2s−1.
Derivation: solving the perpendicular momentum balance

Step: Write the perpendicular linear system

For 𝑩=𝐵𝒛̂, arrange the steady perpendicular momentum equation as

( 𝜈 𝑠 − Ω 𝑠 Ω 𝑠 𝜈 𝑠 ) ( 𝑢 𝑠 , 𝑥 𝑢 𝑠 , 𝑦 ) = ( 𝑞 𝑠 𝑚 𝑠 ) ( 𝐸 𝑥 𝐸 𝑦 ) − 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 𝑚 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 ( 𝜕 𝑛 𝑠 𝜕 𝑥 𝜕 𝑛 𝑠 𝜕 𝑦 ) .

Step: Invert the drag–gyro matrix

Its inverse is

1 𝜈 𝑠 2 + Ω 𝑠 2 ( 𝜈 𝑠 Ω 𝑠 − Ω 𝑠 𝜈 𝑠 ) .

Step: Read off the diffusion tensor

The gradient part of the flux has diagonal coefficient

𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 𝜈 𝑠 𝑚 𝑠 ( 𝜈 𝑠 2 + Ω 𝑠 2 ) = 𝐷 𝑠 𝜈 𝑠 2 𝜈 𝑠 2 + Ω 𝑠 2 ,

and signed off-diagonal coefficient

𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 Ω 𝑠 𝑚 𝑠 ( 𝜈 𝑠 2 + Ω 𝑠 2 ) = 𝐷 𝑠 𝜈 𝑠 Ω 𝑠 𝜈 𝑠 2 + Ω 𝑠 2 .

These are the displayed 𝐷𝑠,⟂ and 𝐷𝑠,𝐻.

Step: Check the strongly magnetized limit

The electric-force part contains the crossed-field drift

𝒖 𝐸 × 𝐵 = 𝑬 × 𝑩 𝐵 2 .

When |Ω𝑠|≫𝜈𝑠,

𝐷 𝑠 , ⟂ ≈ 𝐷 𝑠 ( 𝜈 𝑠 Ω 𝑠 ) 2 .

Thus 𝜈𝑠→0 suppresses classical cross-field diffusion: without interruptions, guiding centers do not make a collisional random walk across field lines.

Step: Relate the coefficient to the thermal gyroradius

With the Chapter 1 convention,

𝑣 th,s = 2 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 𝑚 𝑠 , 𝜌 th,s = 𝑣 th,s | Ω 𝑠 | .

Therefore

𝜌 th,s 2 = 2 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 𝑚 𝑠 Ω 𝑠 2 , 𝐷 𝑠 , ⟂ ≈ ( 𝜈 𝑠 2 ) 𝜌 th,s 2 .

An alternative one-dimensional scale would give 𝐷𝑠,⟂≈𝜈𝑠𝜌1D,s2; it is not the thermal gyroradius convention used elsewhere in this script.

For constant field direction 𝒃 and constant 𝐷𝑠,𝐻, the Hall-like gradient flux 𝐷𝑠,𝐻𝒃×𝛁(𝑛𝑠) has zero divergence, since mixed spatial derivatives commute. It does not by itself broaden a density packet. Spatial variation of the coefficient or field direction, and boundary fluxes, can change that conclusion.

The formal collisionless limit requires care. At fixed nonzero magnetic field, the classical perpendicular coefficient tends to zero. The parallel formula instead grows as 1/𝜈𝑠; once the mean free path is comparable to the system length, local diffusion along the field no longer applies. Particles then retain memory of their motion and boundaries.

Knowledge check

  1. Why is diffusion along a uniform magnetic field not reduced in the same way as cross-field diffusion?
    Answer Gyromotion constrains only the perpendicular plane. Along the field, particles can continue their collisional random walk, so 𝐷 ∥ = 𝐷 𝑠 in this local model.
  2. What dimensionless parameter measures magnetization?
    Answer The ratio | Ω 𝑠 | / 𝜈 𝑠 compares the gyration rate with the collision rate. It is the control parameter in the tensor coefficients.
  3. What happens to classical cross-field diffusion as collisions vanish?
    Answer For fixed 𝐵 it tends to zero as 𝐷 ⟂ ≈ 𝐷 𝑠 ( 𝜈 𝑠 / Ω 𝑠 ) 2 . A collisionless particle remains on its guiding-center orbit apart from other drifts.
  4. What is the physical distinction between 𝐷 ⟂ and 𝐷 𝐻 ?
    Answer 𝐷 ⟂ multiplies the gradient-aligned dissipative flux, whereas the signed 𝐷 𝐻 term rotates the response in the perpendicular plane. The latter is Hall-like and changes sign with charge.

11.5 Fully ionized diffusion and characteristic timescales

In a fully ionized plasma, Coulomb collisions couple the species and the electromagnetic force balance must be used consistently. The classical cross-field coefficient follows from pressure balance and conductivity. Experiments can show faster, anomalous transport, often summarized by the empirical Bohm scaling.

Learning objectives

  • derive the classical fully ionized cross-field coefficient from force balance
  • state the magnetic-field scaling of classical and Bohm-like transport
  • use a diffusion coefficient to estimate a loss time
  • separate a derived collisional result from an empirical turbulent law
Unit ledger The classical and Bohm-like diffusion coefficients are in m2s−1.

The coefficient here describes particle transport. It is different from the magnetic diffusivity 𝐷𝐵 of Chapter 9, which describes resistive smoothing of the magnetic field. In 𝐷⟂𝐵 above, the superscript labels the Bohm estimate. The identical diffusion units do not make these coefficients interchangeable.

Derivation: pressure-driven classical flux

Step: Combine force balance with Ohm’s law

Start from

𝟎 = − 𝛁 ( 𝑝 ) + 𝒋 × 𝑩

and substitute

𝒋 = 𝜎 ( 𝑬 + 𝒖 × 𝑩 ) .

Using

( 𝒖 × 𝑩 ) × 𝑩 = − 𝐵 2 𝒖 ⟂

gives

𝟎 = − 𝛁 ⟂ ( 𝑝 ) + 𝜎 ( 𝑬 × 𝑩 ) − 𝜎 𝐵 2 𝒖 ⟂ .

Step: Solve for the perpendicular velocity

Rearranging gives

𝒖 ⟂ = 𝑬 × 𝑩 𝐵 2 − 𝛁 ⟂ ( 𝑝 ) 𝜎 𝐵 2 .

Step: Identify the pressure-driven diffusion

Multiply by 𝑛. The first term is a common crossed-field drift and does not diffuse a uniform density. For an isothermal two-temperature hydrogen plasma,

𝛁 ⟂ ( 𝑝 ) = 𝑘 𝐵 ( 𝑇 𝑒 + 𝑇 𝑖 ) 𝛁 ⟂ ( 𝑛 ) .

The pressure term in the particle flux is consequently

𝚪 ⟂ diff = − 𝑛 𝑘 𝐵 ( 𝑇 𝑒 + 𝑇 𝑖 ) 𝛁 ⟂ ( 𝑛 ) 𝜎 𝐵 2 .

Therefore

𝐷 ⟂ cl = 𝑛 𝑘 𝐵 ( 𝑇 𝑒 + 𝑇 𝑖 ) 𝜎 𝐵 2 = 𝜂 𝑛 𝑘 𝐵 ( 𝑇 𝑒 + 𝑇 𝑖 ) 𝐵 2 .

The factor 1/𝐵2 arises because the field enters twice: once through the Lorentz force density 𝒋×𝑩 and once through the motional term 𝒖×𝑩 in Ohm’s law.

Step: State the comparison with anomalous transport

The classical loss time for a macroscopic length 𝐿 is

𝜏 D = 𝐿 2 𝐷 ⟂ cl .

The Bohm expression is not obtained by this force-balance derivation; it is a phenomenological comparison motivated by turbulent or anomalous transport. Neoclassical calculations add field geometry, trapped-particle orbits, and finite collisionality.

Knowledge check

  1. Why does the classical diffusion coefficient scale as 𝐵 − 2 rather than 𝐵 − 1 ?
    Answer The field enters through both 𝒋 × 𝑩 in force balance and 𝒖 × 𝑩 in Ohm’s law. Solving the perpendicular balance therefore produces the factor 1 / ( 𝜎 𝐵 2 ) in 𝐷 ⟂ cl .
  2. What magnetic-field scaling distinguishes classical from Bohm-like diffusion?
    Answer The local classical result scales as 𝐵 − 2 , whereas the empirical Bohm estimate scales as 𝐵 − 1 at fixed temperature.
  3. How is a diffusion loss time estimated from a coefficient?
    Answer For a characteristic length 𝐿 , use 𝜏 D = 𝐿 2 / 𝐷 . Geometry changes the numerical factor, but not the basic dimensional scaling.
  4. Why should a Bohm coefficient be labelled empirical?
    Answer Its 𝐵 − 1 dependence summarizes anomalous transport linked to turbulence; it is not derived from the local collisional force balance used for 𝐷 ⟂ cl .
