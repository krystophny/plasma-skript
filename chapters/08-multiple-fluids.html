8. Multiple-fluid theory of plasmas

A plasma can contain several interpenetrating fluids at the same position. Electrons and ions feel the same fields but have different masses, charges, temperatures, and collision rates. Keeping their equations separate makes those differences explicit before a one-fluid approximation is chosen.

8.1 Species-resolved plasma variables

When is one bulk velocity insufficient? It is insufficient whenever electrons and ions carry different momentum or respond differently to a force. The first step is therefore to define one set of fluid variables for each species.

Learning objectives

  • define the species variables needed by a two-fluid model
  • distinguish quasi-neutrality from equality of species velocities
  • identify the common electromagnetic coupling and species-specific terms
  • trace the information retained when kinetic distributions become fluids

The species moments from the kinetic description are

𝜌 𝑠 = 𝑚 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 , 𝜌 𝑞 = ∑ 𝑠 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 , 𝒋 = ∑ 𝑠 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠

The mass density 𝜌𝑠 is in kgm−3, charge density 𝜌𝑞 is in Cm−3, and 𝒋 is the total current density in Am−2. The charge 𝑒 is measured in C. In a quasi-neutral hydrogen plasma, 𝑛𝑒≈𝑛𝑖, but generally 𝒖𝑒≠𝒖𝑖; the difference is precisely what permits a current.

Derivation: species moments retain separate fluids

Step: Take moments species by species

Begin with a distribution 𝑓𝑠(𝑡,𝒓,𝒗) for every species. Its zeroth and first velocity moments are

𝑛 𝑠 = ∫ 𝑓 𝑠 d 3 𝒗 , 𝑛 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 = ∫ 𝒗 𝑓 𝑠 d 3 𝒗 .

Multiplication by 𝑚𝑠 or 𝑞𝑠 gives the species mass and current contributions.

Step: Form total variables only after the species moments

Because the moments are taken before summing over 𝑠, electron and ion velocities remain independent fields. Only after those fields have been defined may one form

𝜌 = ∑ 𝑠 𝜌 𝑠 , 𝒋 = ∑ 𝑠 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 .

A mass-weighted bulk velocity is another derived quantity.

Step: Separate quasi-neutrality from current

Quasi-neutrality constrains only the zeroth charge moment:

𝜌 𝑞 = ∑ 𝑠 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 ≈ 0 .

It does not set the first moments equal. For hydrogen,

𝜌 𝑞 ≈ 𝑒 ( 𝑛 𝑖 − 𝑛 𝑒 ) , 𝒋 = 𝑒 𝑛 𝑖 𝒖 𝑖 − 𝑒 𝑛 𝑒 𝒖 𝑒 .

The current can therefore remain finite even when the charge density is small.

Knowledge check

  1. Which species variables are retained by a two-fluid model?
    Answer At minimum, each species has 𝑛 𝑠 , 𝒖 𝑠 , and a pressure model 𝑷 𝑠 , together with fields and collision or source terms.
  2. What does quasi-neutrality constrain?
    Answer It constrains the charge density 𝜌 𝑞 = ∑ 𝑠 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 to be small in the adopted ordering. It does not require 𝒖 𝑒 = 𝒖 𝑖 .
  3. Why can a quasi-neutral plasma carry a current?
    Answer Because the current is 𝒋 = ∑ 𝑠 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 . Equal and opposite charge densities can be accompanied by different species velocities.
  4. What is the physical role of the magnetic field in the species equations?
    Answer It couples to each species through 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 ( 𝒖 𝑠 × 𝑩 ) . The force is perpendicular to the instantaneous velocity and changes direction of motion without directly doing work.

8.2 Complete two-fluid equations

What must be evolved once the species variables are defined? Each species needs particle conservation, momentum transport, and an energy or pressure closure. Maxwell’s equations provide the shared fields and the coupling between the species currents and charge density.

Learning objectives

  • write the conservative continuity, momentum, and energy equations
  • identify the Lorentz force and collision moments in each species equation
  • state the Maxwell equations that close the field coupling
  • separate exact moment equations from pressure and collision closures
Unit ledger The Lorentz force density is in Nm−3, because 𝑞𝑠𝑛𝑠𝑬 and 𝑞𝑠𝑛𝑠(𝒖𝑠×𝑩) have that unit. The pressure divergence and 𝑹𝑠 are also force densities.

The species continuity equation is

𝜕 𝑛 𝑠 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝛁 · ( 𝑛 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 ) = 0

In conservative momentum form,

𝜕 𝜌 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝛁 · ( 𝜌 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 + 𝑷 𝑠 ) = 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 ( 𝑬 + 𝒖 𝑠 × 𝑩 ) + 𝑹 𝑠

Equivalently, after using continuity, the left-hand side can be written as the species material derivative:

𝜌 𝑠 ( 𝜕 𝒖 𝑠 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝒖 𝑠 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝒖 𝑠 ) ) = 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 ( 𝑬 + 𝒖 𝑠 × 𝑩 ) − 𝛁 · ( 𝑷 𝑠 ) + 𝑹 𝑠

The momentum density is in kgm−2s−1. Every term in the material equation is a force density in Nm−3. The tensor divergence becomes 𝛁(𝑝𝑠) only for an isotropic pressure tensor.

As in Chapter 7, 𝒘𝑠=𝒗−𝒖𝑠 is the random velocity relative to the species flow. Define the total species kinetic-energy density and heat-flux vector by

𝐸 kin , 𝑠 = 𝜌 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 2 2 + 𝜀 𝑠 , 𝜀 𝑠 = 𝑚 𝑠 ∫ 𝒘 𝑠 2 𝑓 𝑠 d 3 𝒗 2 𝒒 ℎ , 𝑠 = 𝑚 𝑠 ∫ 𝒘 𝑠 2 𝒘 𝑠 𝑓 𝑠 d 3 𝒗 2

The subscript ℎ labels heat flux, distinguishing this vector from particle charge 𝑞𝑠. The energy 𝐸kin,𝑠 contains bulk and random kinetic energy; electromagnetic field energy is separate.

The corresponding energy balance is

𝜕 𝐸 kin , 𝑠 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝛁 · ( 𝐸 kin , 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 + 𝑷 𝑠 ⋅ 𝒖 𝑠 + 𝒒 ℎ , 𝑠 ) = 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 ⋅ 𝑬 + 𝑄 𝑠

The field equations are

𝛁 · ( 𝑬 ) = 𝜌 𝑞 𝜀 0 , 𝛁 · ( 𝑩 ) = 0 𝛁 × ( 𝑬 ) = − 𝜕 𝑩 𝜕 𝑡 , 𝛁 × ( 𝑩 ) = 𝜇 0 𝒋 + 𝜇 0 𝜀 0 𝜕 𝑬 𝜕 𝑡
Derivation: from the kinetic equation to the fluid balances

Step: Start with the conservative kinetic equation

Use

𝜕 𝑓 𝑠 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝛁 · ( 𝑓 𝑠 𝒗 ) + 𝛁 · ( 𝑓 𝑠 𝒂 𝑠 ) = 𝐶 𝑠 [ 𝑓 ] ,

with

𝒂 𝑠 = 𝑞 𝑠 𝑚 𝑠 ( 𝑬 + 𝒗 × 𝑩 ) .

Step: Take the zeroth moment

Integrating over velocity gives 𝜕𝑛𝑠/𝜕𝑡 from the time term and 𝛁·(𝑛𝑠𝒖𝑠) from spatial streaming. The velocity divergence is a vanishing surface term, and particle-conserving collisions have zero zeroth moment. This gives the species continuity equation.

Step: Take the first moment

Multiply by 𝑚𝑠𝒗 and integrate. In components, spatial streaming gives

𝑚 𝑠 ∫ 𝑣 𝑖 𝜕 ( 𝑣 𝑗 𝑓 𝑠 ) 𝜕 𝑥 𝑗 d 3 𝒗 = 𝜕 ( 𝑚 𝑠 ∫ 𝑣 𝑖 𝑣 𝑗 𝑓 𝑠 d 3 𝒗 ) 𝜕 𝑥 𝑗 .

Writing 𝒗=𝒖𝑠+𝒘𝑠 decomposes the raw tensor into

𝜌 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 + 𝑷 𝑠 .

Step: Evaluate the Lorentz and collision moments

Integration by parts in velocity gives

𝑚 𝑠 ∫ 𝑣 𝑖 𝜕 ( 𝑎 𝑠 , 𝑗 𝑓 𝑠 ) 𝜕 𝑣 𝑗 d 3 𝒗 = − 𝑚 𝑠 ∫ 𝑎 𝑠 , 𝑖 𝑓 𝑠 d 3 𝒗 .

Moving this term to the right gives

𝑚 𝑠 ∫ 𝒂 𝑠 𝑓 𝑠 d 3 𝒗 = 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝑬 + 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 ( 𝒖 𝑠 × 𝑩 ) .

The remaining collision moment is

𝑹 𝑠 = 𝑚 𝑠 ∫ 𝒗 𝐶 𝑠 [ 𝑓 ] d 3 𝒗 .

Step: Take the energy moment

Multiply by 𝑚𝑠𝑣2/2. After integration by parts, the force work is

− 𝑚 𝑠 ∫ 𝒗 ⋅ 𝒂 𝑠 𝑓 𝑠 d 3 𝒗 .

The magnetic contribution vanishes because 𝒗⋅(𝒗×𝑩)=0, while the electric contribution is 𝑞𝑠𝑛𝑠𝒖𝑠⋅𝑬.

Splitting

𝑣 2 = 𝒖 𝑠 2 + 2 𝒖 𝑠 ⋅ 𝒘 𝑠 + 𝒘 𝑠 2

separates directed kinetic energy, internal energy, pressure work, and heat flux. The collision energy moment is

𝑄 𝑠 = ( 𝑚 𝑠 2 ) ∫ 𝑣 2 𝐶 𝑠 [ 𝑓 ] d 3 𝒗 .

No pressure equation has been assumed. Replacing 𝑷𝑠 by a scalar or 𝒒ℎ,𝑠 by a constitutive law is a separate closure choice.

  1. Which term couples a species momentum equation to the electromagnetic field?
    Answer The Lorentz force density 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 ( 𝑬 + 𝒖 𝑠 × 𝑩 ) couples the species momentum to the common fields.
  2. When can the pressure-tensor divergence be written as a pressure gradient?
    Answer When the pressure tensor is isotropic, 𝑷 𝑠 = 𝑝 𝑠 𝑰 , so that 𝛁 · ( 𝑷 𝑠 ) = 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑠 ) . Isotropy is an additional model assumption.
  3. Why does the magnetic force not appear in the species energy source?
    Answer Because 𝒖 𝑠 ⋅ ( 𝒖 𝑠 × 𝑩 ) = 0 . The magnetic force changes direction but does no direct work; the electric field contributes 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 ⋅ 𝑬 .
  4. What must be specified in addition to the exact moment balances to obtain a usable fluid model?
    Answer One must choose pressure and heat-flux closures and specify collision or source models. Those choices state which kinetic information has been discarded.

8.3 Perpendicular drift balance

How does a pressure gradient move a magnetized species? On scales longer than its gyroradius, the perpendicular momentum equation can balance the pressure force against the Lorentz force. Solving that local balance reveals a common electric drift and a species-dependent diamagnetic drift.

Learning objectives

  • state the ordering behind a local perpendicular force balance
  • derive the electric and pressure-gradient drift velocities
  • track the sign of the diamagnetic drift for electrons and ions
  • identify the terms that invalidate the low-inertia drift ordering

Let 𝒃=𝑩/𝐵 and project the species momentum balance perpendicular to the field:

0 ≈ 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 ( 𝑬 ⟂ + 𝒖 𝑠 , ⟂ × 𝑩 ) − 𝛁 ⟂ 𝑝 𝑠

Here 𝑬⟂=𝑬−𝒃(𝒃⋅𝑬) and 𝛁⟂=𝛁−𝒃(𝒃⋅𝛁) are perpendicular projections. Solving for the perpendicular velocity gives

𝒖 𝑠 , ⟂ = 𝒖 𝐸 × 𝐵 + 𝒖 ∗ , 𝑠 𝒖 𝐸 × 𝐵 = 𝑬 × 𝑩 𝐵 2 𝒖 ∗ , 𝑠 = 𝑩 × 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑠 ) 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝐵 2

Both velocities are in ms−1. The electric drift is independent of species, while the diamagnetic drift changes sign with 𝑞𝑠 and depends on the species pressure gradient. The star labels the diamagnetic contribution; it is not a complex conjugate. If the pressure is uniform, 𝒖∗,𝑠=𝟎.

Derivation: solve the perpendicular force balance

Step: Write the perpendicular force balance

Start with

𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 ( 𝑬 ⟂ + 𝒖 𝑠 , ⟂ × 𝑩 ) = 𝛁 ⟂ 𝑝 𝑠 .

Step: Eliminate the magnetic cross product

Cross the equation with 𝑩 from the right. The identity

( 𝒖 × 𝑩 ) × 𝑩 = − 𝐵 2 𝒖 ⟂

gives

𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝑬 × 𝑩 − 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝐵 2 𝒖 𝑠 , ⟂ = 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑠 ) × 𝑩 .

Step: Separate the two perpendicular drifts

Rearrange and use 𝛁(𝑝)×𝑩=−𝑩×𝛁(𝑝):

𝒖 𝑠 , ⟂ = 𝑬 × 𝑩 𝐵 2 + 𝑩 × 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑠 ) 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝐵 2 .

The first term is the common electric drift because 𝑞𝑠 cancels from the electric force balance. The second is the charge-dependent diamagnetic drift. The spatial ordering requires the pressure-gradient scale 𝐿⟂ to be much larger than the species gyroradius 𝑟𝐿,𝑠, both measured in metres:

𝑟 𝐿 , 𝑠 𝐿 ⟂ ≪ 1 .

This dimensionless inequality supports a local orbit average. Neglecting inertia also requires slow evolution and small convective acceleration relative to the retained forces, as assumed above. Here 𝑟𝐿,𝑠 denotes orbit size; 𝜌𝑠 continues to denote mass density.

Knowledge check

  1. Which term makes the electric drift common to both species?
    Answer The electric force is proportional to 𝑞 𝑠 , which cancels the 1 / 𝑞 𝑠 introduced when the perpendicular Lorentz balance is solved. Thus 𝒖 𝐸 × 𝐵 = 𝑬 × 𝑩 / 𝐵 2 .
  2. How does reversing the charge affect the diamagnetic drift?
    Answer It reverses 𝒖 ∗ , 𝑠 because the drift is proportional to 1 / 𝑞 𝑠 . The pressure gradient and magnetic-field directions remain unchanged.
  3. What scale ordering supports the local drift balance?
    Answer The pressure-gradient scale must be much larger than the species gyroradius, and perpendicular inertia and collisions must be small compared with the Lorentz and pressure forces.
  4. What happens to the pressure-gradient drift when 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑠 ) = 𝟎 ?
    Answer It vanishes. The species can still share a nonzero electric 𝐸 × 𝐵 drift if a perpendicular electric field is present.

8.4 Diamagnetic current

Why can opposite particle drifts produce one current direction? Current is charge-weighted flow. The charge sign that reverses the electron drift is multiplied once more when the electron contribution to the current is formed, so the two species contributions add.

Learning objectives

  • sum the species drift currents in a quasi-neutral plasma
  • show why the common electric drift contributes little net current
  • relate the diamagnetic current to the total pressure gradient
  • distinguish current response from bulk mass transport

Insert the perpendicular drift into the current definition:

𝒋 ⟂ = ∑ 𝑠 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 , ⟂ = 𝜌 𝑞 ( 𝑬 × 𝑩 ) 𝐵 2 + ∑ 𝑠 ( 𝑩 × 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑠 ) ) 𝐵 2

Under quasi-neutrality, the first term is small and the pressure term is

𝒋 ∗ = 𝑩 × 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑒 + 𝑝 𝑖 ) 𝐵 2

For scalar ideal-gas pressures, 𝑝𝑠=𝑛𝑠𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑠. If 𝑛𝑒≈𝑛𝑖≈𝑛, then

𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑒 + 𝑝 𝑖 ) = 𝑘 𝐵 ( ( 𝑇 𝑒 + 𝑇 𝑖 ) 𝛁 ( 𝑛 ) + 𝑛 𝛁 ( 𝑇 𝑒 + 𝑇 𝑖 ) )

The diamagnetic current is perpendicular to both 𝑩 and the total pressure gradient. A pressure gradient can therefore drive a current even while the leading-order charge density remains nearly zero.

For example, take uniform 𝑩=𝐵𝒆𝑧, no electric field, constant temperature, and a density increasing with 𝑥 over many gyroradii. More gyro-orbits centered on the dense side cross a given point than do orbits centered on the dilute side. Their opposite local velocities therefore do not cancel: positive ions have a mean velocity along +𝒆𝑦 and electrons along −𝒆𝑦. The guiding centers have no perpendicular drift in this uniform field. The local velocity moment records the imbalance of orbit crossings, not migration of their centers.

Derivation: add the charge-weighted drift responses

Step: Charge-weight one species

For each species, the diamagnetic contribution obeys

𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝒖 ∗ , 𝑠 = 𝑩 × 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑠 ) 𝐵 2 .

The factors 𝑞𝑠𝑛𝑠 cancel the denominator in the species velocity.

Step: Sum the diamagnetic currents

Summing over electrons and ions gives

𝒋 ∗ = 𝑩 × [ 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑒 ) + 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑖 ) ] 𝐵 2 .

The pressure-drift velocities are opposite for opposite charges, but their charge-weighted currents add.

Step: Check the common electric drift

The common drift instead gives

∑ 𝑠 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝒖 𝐸 × 𝐵 = 𝜌 𝑞 𝒖 𝐸 × 𝐵 .

It vanishes at leading order when 𝜌𝑞≈0. This cancellation is different from the diamagnetic sum because the electric drift is the same for both species.

Step: Interpret the result as a current response

The formula does not by itself assert that the center of mass moves across the pressure gradient. In a stationary, locally ordered configuration, diamagnetic flow can be divergence-free. Whether it transports mass or changes the pressure profile requires the full continuity and momentum equations, boundary conditions, and the terms omitted in the local balance.

8.5 Parallel force balance and the one-fluid limit

What remains after perpendicular drifts have been separated? Along the magnetic field, the Lorentz force has no magnetic component. Parallel pressure gradients, electric fields, inertia, and collisions therefore determine the species response and provide the bridge to one-fluid models.

Learning objectives

  • project the species momentum equation along the magnetic field
  • derive the isothermal electron Boltzmann response as a limiting case
  • define total mass velocity, pressure, charge, and current variables
  • identify the relative-flow stress retained when species equations are summed

Projecting the species momentum equation gives

𝜌 𝑠 ( 𝜕 𝑢 ∥ , 𝑠 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝒖 𝑠 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝑢 ∥ , 𝑠 ) ) = − 𝒃 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑠 ) + 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝐸 ∥ + 𝑅 ∥ , 𝑠 , 𝐸 ∥ = 𝒃 ⋅ 𝑬

For inertialess, collisionless electrons with 𝑞𝑒=−𝑒,

0 = − 𝒃 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑒 ) − 𝑒 𝑛 𝑒 𝐸 ∥

With 𝑝𝑒=𝑛𝑒𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑒 and uniform 𝑇𝑒, this becomes

𝐸 ∥ = − ( 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 𝑒 ) 𝒃 ⋅ 𝛁 ( ln 𝑛 𝑒 ) , 𝐸 ∥ = − 𝒃 ⋅ 𝛁 ( Φ )

and integration along a field line gives the electron Boltzmann relation

𝑛 𝑒 = 𝑛 𝑒 , 0 exp ( 𝑒 ( Φ − Φ 0 ) 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 )

Here 𝑛𝑒,0 is the density at a reference point with potential Φ0 on the same field line. Parallel balance fixes the density variation along that line; it does not set a common reference density for all field lines.

The exact one-fluid definitions are

𝜌 = ∑ 𝑠 𝜌 𝑠 , 𝜌 𝒖 = ∑ 𝑠 𝜌 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 , 𝑷 1 = ∑ 𝑠 𝑷 𝑠 + ∑ 𝑠 𝜌 𝑠 𝑽 𝑠 𝑽 𝑠 𝜌 𝑞 = ∑ 𝑠 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 , 𝒋 = ∑ 𝑠 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 , 𝑽 𝑠 = 𝒖 𝑠 − 𝒖

Here 𝑷1 includes both random pressure and relative-flow stress. Adding the species momentum equations then yields

𝜕 𝜌 𝒖 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝛁 · ( 𝜌 𝒖 𝒖 + 𝑷 1 ) = 𝜌 𝑞 𝑬 + 𝒋 × 𝑩 + ∑ 𝑠 𝑹 𝑠

For isolated elastic collisions, the last term vanishes. Dropping the relative-flow part of 𝑷1 is an additional one-fluid closure, not a consequence of adding the equations.

Derivation: parallel balance and species summation

Step: Project one species along the field

Dot the material momentum equation with 𝒃. Since 𝒃⋅(𝒖𝑠×𝑩)=0, the magnetic force disappears. The projected pressure, electric, and collision terms are

− 𝒃 ⋅ 𝛁 · ( 𝑷 𝑠 ) , 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝐸 ∥ , 𝑅 ∥ , 𝑠 = 𝒃 ⋅ 𝑹 𝑠 .

For scalar pressure, the first term becomes −𝒃⋅𝛁(𝑝𝑠).

Step: Derive the electron Boltzmann response

Set electron inertia and collisions to zero and use 𝑞𝑒=−𝑒:

𝒃 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑒 ) = − 𝑒 𝑛 𝑒 𝐸 ∥ .

Isothermal pressure gives 𝒃⋅𝛁(𝑝𝑒)=𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑒𝒃⋅𝛁(𝑛𝑒), so

𝐸 ∥ = − ( 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 𝑒 ) 𝒃 ⋅ 𝛁 ( ln 𝑛 𝑒 ) .

With 𝐸∥=−𝒃⋅𝛁(Φ),

𝒃 ⋅ 𝛁 ( ln 𝑛 𝑒 − 𝑒 Φ 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 ) = 0 .

Along a connected field line,

𝑛 𝑒 = 𝑛 𝑒 , 0 exp ( 𝑒 ( Φ − Φ 0 ) 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 ) .

Step: Sum the species momentum equations

Write

𝒖 𝑠 = 𝒖 + 𝑽 𝑠 .

Then

∑ 𝑠 𝜌 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 = 𝜌 𝒖 𝒖 + ∑ 𝑠 𝜌 𝑠 𝑽 𝑠 𝑽 𝑠 ,

because

∑ 𝑠 𝜌 𝑠 𝑽 𝑠 = ∑ 𝑠 𝜌 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 − 𝜌 𝒖 = 𝟎 .

The field forces become

∑ 𝑠 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝑬 = 𝜌 𝑞 𝑬 , ∑ 𝑠 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 ( 𝒖 𝑠 × 𝑩 ) = 𝒋 × 𝑩 .

These identities give the summed momentum equation. If interspecies collisions are internal, their momentum sources cancel in the sum.

Knowledge check

  1. Why does the magnetic force disappear from the parallel momentum equation?
    Answer The magnetic force is proportional to 𝒖 𝑠 × 𝑩 and is perpendicular to 𝑩 , so its dot product with 𝒃 is zero.
  2. Which assumptions produce the electron Boltzmann relation?
    Answer

    Negligible parallel electron inertia and collisions, scalar isothermal pressure, electrostatic parallel balance, and a connected field line lead to

    𝑛 𝑒 𝑛 𝑒 , 0 = exp ( 𝑒 ( Φ − Φ 0 ) 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 ) .
  3. What is the one-fluid velocity?
    Answer It is the mass-weighted velocity defined by 𝜌 𝒖 = ∑ 𝑠 𝜌 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 , with 𝜌 = ∑ 𝑠 𝜌 𝑠 .
  4. What relative-flow contribution appears in the summed momentum flux?
    Answer The tensor ∑ 𝑠 𝜌 𝑠 𝑽 𝑠 𝑽 𝑠 , where 𝑽 𝑠 = 𝒖 𝑠 − 𝒖 , remains in the total pressure tensor. It can be neglected only under an additional ordering.
