13. Waves in cold magnetized plasmas

A static magnetic field turns the isotropic plasma response into a tensor. This chapter derives that tensor from the cold-fluid momentum equation and uses it to organize parallel, perpendicular, and oblique wave propagation. Propagation direction, polarization, and frequency ordering are carried together throughout, following the standard cold-plasma treatment in [12].

13.1 Cold magnetized response and the dielectric tensor

How does a uniform magnetic field enter a linear wave calculation? Start with the cold momentum equation for each species, solve the transverse velocity response, and convert the current into a dielectric tensor. Once this tensor is known, Maxwell’s equations supply the wave matrix.

Learning objectives

  • state the cold, homogeneous, collisionless magnetized ordering
  • derive the species current response from the linear momentum equation
  • identify the transverse and parallel dielectric coefficients
  • write the Maxwell wave matrix and its determinant condition
Unit ledger The dielectric coefficients and refractive index 𝑁=𝑘𝑐/𝜔 are dimensionless.

Each tensor column gives the response to one electric-field component. The coefficients 𝜀⟂ and 𝜀∥ describe response perpendicular and parallel to the background magnetic field; 𝜀× couples the two perpendicular directions. The symbol × in its subscript is a label, not an instruction to take another cross product. The identity part is the vacuum response, and the remaining terms describe the induced plasma current.

Derivation: from cold momentum to the dielectric tensor

Step: Resolve the cold momentum equation

The linearized cold momentum equation is

− 𝑖 𝜔 𝑚 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 , 1 = 𝑞 𝑠 ( 𝑬 1 + 𝒖 𝑠 , 1 × 𝑩 0 ) .

With 𝑩0=𝐵0𝒆𝑧, the transverse components form a coupled two-by-two system:

− 𝑖 𝜔 𝑢 𝑠 , 1 , 𝑥 − Ω 𝑠 𝑢 𝑠 , 1 , 𝑦 = ( 𝑞 𝑠 𝑚 𝑠 ) 𝐸 1 , 𝑥

and

Ω 𝑠 𝑢 𝑠 , 1 , 𝑥 − 𝑖 𝜔 𝑢 𝑠 , 1 , 𝑦 = ( 𝑞 𝑠 𝑚 𝑠 ) 𝐸 1 , 𝑦 .

The parallel component is independent:

− 𝑖 𝜔 𝑢 𝑠 , 1 , 𝑧 = ( 𝑞 𝑠 𝑚 𝑠 ) 𝐸 1 , 𝑧 .

Step: Invert the transverse response

Inverting the transverse system gives

𝑢 𝑠 , 1 , 𝑥 = ( 𝑖 𝑞 𝑠 𝜔 𝑚 𝑠 ( 𝜔 2 − Ω 𝑠 2 ) ) 𝐸 1 , 𝑥 − ( 𝑞 𝑠 Ω 𝑠 𝑚 𝑠 ( 𝜔 2 − Ω 𝑠 2 ) ) 𝐸 1 , 𝑦 ,

and

𝑢 𝑠 , 1 , 𝑦 = ( 𝑞 𝑠 Ω 𝑠 𝑚 𝑠 ( 𝜔 2 − Ω 𝑠 2 ) ) 𝐸 1 , 𝑥 + ( 𝑖 𝑞 𝑠 𝜔 𝑚 𝑠 ( 𝜔 2 − Ω 𝑠 2 ) ) 𝐸 1 , 𝑦 .

Multiply by 𝑞𝑠𝑛𝑠,0 and sum over species to obtain 𝒋1. The transverse entries are coupled because the Lorentz force rotates the velocity in the 𝑥−𝑦 plane; the parallel motion has no magnetic force.

Step: Define the dielectric response

Insert the current into

𝜺 𝑝 ⋅ 𝑬 1 = 𝑬 1 + ( 𝑖 𝜔 𝜀 0 ) 𝒋 1 .

Reading off the coefficients gives the cold dielectric tensor displayed above. Its off-diagonal entries encode the rotation of transverse motion.

Step: Insert the response into Maxwell’s equations

Fourier-transformed Faraday and Ampere laws are

𝒌 × 𝑬 1 = 𝜔 𝑩 1

and

𝒌 × 𝑩 1 = − 𝜔 𝑬 1 𝑐 2 − 𝑖 𝜇 0 𝒋 1 .

Substitute the first relation into the second, eliminate 𝒋1 by the dielectric definition with 𝜇0𝜀0=1/𝑐2, and use

𝒌 × ( 𝒌 × 𝑬 1 ) = 𝒌 ( 𝒌 ⋅ 𝑬 1 ) − 𝑘 2 𝑬 1 .

A nonzero field amplitude requires the determinant of the resulting wave matrix to vanish.

Knowledge check

  1. Which assumption makes the cold-fluid momentum equation algebraic in Fourier space?
    Answer A homogeneous equilibrium together with the plane-wave ansatz replaces derivatives by multiplication by − 𝑖 𝜔 and 𝑖 𝒌 . The cold ordering removes pressure gradients and the collisionless ordering removes drag terms.
  2. Why are the two transverse electric components coupled?
    Answer The transverse velocity crossed with 𝑩 0 points into the other transverse direction. The Lorentz force therefore couples the 𝑥 and 𝑦 momentum equations and creates the off-diagonal dielectric coefficient 𝜀 × .
  3. Why is the parallel dielectric coefficient different from the transverse coefficient?
    Answer The magnetic force vanishes for motion parallel to 𝑩 0 . Parallel motion therefore responds with the unmagnetized factor 1 − ∑ 𝑠 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑠 2 / 𝜔 2 , while transverse motion contains the gyrofrequency denominators.
  4. What mathematical condition selects a wave mode?
    Answer The wave amplitudes satisfy a homogeneous matrix equation. A nonzero electric-field vector exists only when the matrix is singular, so its determinant must vanish.

13.2 Parallel propagation and circular polarization

When the wave travels along the magnetic field, the longitudinal and transverse problems separate. The transverse block is diagonalized by circular polarization: the two rotation senses see different effective dielectric coefficients, while the longitudinal branch remains a local plasma oscillation in the cold limit.

Learning objectives

  • factor the parallel wave matrix into longitudinal and circular branches
  • derive the two circular-mode dispersion relations
  • calculate the cutoff frequencies and identify the cyclotron resonance
  • explain how different circular wave numbers rotate linear polarization
Unit ledger The refractive index 𝑁=𝑘𝑐/𝜔, normalized frequency is 𝜔̂=𝜔/𝜔𝑝,𝑒, and magnetization is 𝑌=𝜔𝑐,𝑒/𝜔𝑝,𝑒; all three are dimensionless. The dimensional 𝑘 is in m−1, 𝜔 and 𝜔𝑐,𝑒 are in s−1, and phase or group velocities are in ms−1.

In these circular-mode formulas, 𝑠 labels polarization, not particle species. The factor −𝑖𝑠 specifies equal amplitudes with a quarter-cycle phase difference between the two electric components. This algebraic convention fixes the rotation sense without relying on an unstated viewing direction.

Derivation: circular factorization and Faraday rotation

Step: Factor the parallel wave matrix

For parallel propagation, divide the wave matrix by 𝜔2/𝑐2:

( 𝜀 ⟂ − 𝑁 2 − 𝑖 𝜀 × 0 𝑖 𝜀 × 𝜀 ⟂ − 𝑁 2 0 0 0 𝜀 ∥ ) ( 𝐸 𝑥 𝐸 𝑦 𝐸 𝑧 ) = ( 0 0 0 ) .

The longitudinal factor gives 𝜀∥=0. The transverse determinant is

( 𝜀 ⟂ − 𝑁 2 ) 2 − 𝜀 × 2 = 0 .

Thus the two transverse eigenvalues are

𝑁 2 = 𝜀 ⟂ + 𝜀 × , 𝑁 2 = 𝜀 ⟂ − 𝜀 × .

Step: Express the circular branches

For an electron, the signed gyrofrequency is negative when 𝐵0 points along positive 𝑧. Use its positive magnitude 𝜔𝑐,𝑒 and the circular basis

𝐸 1 , 𝑦 = − 𝑖 𝑠 𝐸 1 , 𝑥 .

The two indices become

𝑁 𝑠 2 = 𝜀 ⟂ − 𝑠 𝜀 × = 1 − 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 2 𝜔 ( 𝜔 + 𝑠 𝜔 𝑐 , 𝑒 ) .

Setting 𝑁𝑠=0 gives

𝜔 2 + 𝑠 𝜔 𝑐 , 𝑒 𝜔 − 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 2 = 0 .

The positive root is the cutoff stated above. For 𝑠=−1, the denominator vanishes at the electron cyclotron frequency. The cold response is then singular, so finite temperature or collisions are required before that limit is interpreted.

Step: Accumulate the relative phase

A linearly polarized wave is the equal-amplitude sum of the two circular eigenmodes. After a distance 𝐿, their relative phase is

( 𝑘 + − 𝑘 − ) 𝐿 .

The major axis rotates by half this phase:

𝜃 𝐹 = ( 𝑘 + − 𝑘 − ) 𝐿 2 .

If the plasma varies slowly along the ray, use the local wave-number difference instead:

𝜃 𝐹 = ∫ ( 𝑘 + ( 𝑧 ) − 𝑘 − ( 𝑧 ) ) d 𝑧 2 .

Faraday rotation compares two circular modes at the same frequency. When both propagate without attenuation and have equal amplitudes, their superposition remains linearly polarized. Different wave numbers rotate its polarization axis with distance; at a fixed position that axis remains fixed as the electric vector oscillates. If one component is evanescent or attenuated differently, the equal-amplitude linear-polarization picture no longer applies.

Knowledge check

  1. Why are circular polarizations the natural eigenvectors for parallel propagation?
    Answer The transverse dielectric block has equal diagonal entries and antisymmetric off-diagonal entries. Its eigenvectors are the two circular combinations of 𝐸 𝑥 and 𝐸 𝑦 , which remove the coupling.
  2. What is the physical meaning of a circular-mode cutoff?
    Answer It is the frequency at which the corresponding refractive index vanishes. The wave number is then zero, so the branch meets the zero-wave-number boundary between propagation and non-propagation.
  3. Which cold-plasma feature identifies the cyclotron-sensitive branch?
    Answer Its denominator contains 𝜔 ( 𝜔 − 𝜔 𝑐 , 𝑒 ) under the chosen convention, so the response becomes singular as 𝜔 approaches the electron cyclotron frequency.
  4. Why does a linearly polarized wave rotate in a magnetized plasma?
    Answer A linear wave is a sum of two circular eigenmodes. Since the modes have different wave numbers, their relative phase changes with distance and the direction of the linear polarization changes.

13.3 Perpendicular propagation: ordinary and extraordinary modes

Turning the wave vector perpendicular to the magnetic field changes which field component is decoupled. The ordinary mode has its electric field parallel to the background field and therefore sees the unmagnetized plasma response. The extraordinary mode has electric components in the plane perpendicular to the background field, including a component along the wave vector, and contains an upper-hybrid resonance.

Learning objectives

  • factor the perpendicular wave matrix into ordinary and extraordinary modes
  • derive the ordinary and extraordinary refractive indices
  • identify the upper-hybrid resonance and the extraordinary polarization
  • distinguish a cutoff from a resonant stopband
Unit ledger The angle 𝜃 is dimensionless and measured in radians. The refractive index 𝑁=𝑘𝑐/𝜔, 𝜔̂=𝜔/𝜔𝑝,𝑒, and 𝑌=𝜔𝑐,𝑒/𝜔𝑝,𝑒 are dimensionless. Dimensional 𝑘 is in m−1, 𝜔 in s−1, and wavelengths in m.
Derivation: perpendicular factorization

Step: Separate the ordinary mode

For 𝒌=𝑘𝒆𝑥, the wave equation is

( 𝜀 ⟂ − 𝑖 𝜀 × 0 𝑖 𝜀 × 𝜀 ⟂ − 𝑁 2 0 0 0 𝜀 ∥ − 𝑁 2 ) ( 𝐸 𝑥 𝐸 𝑦 𝐸 𝑧 ) = ( 0 0 0 ) .

The 𝑧 equation is independent of the 𝑥−𝑦 block. A nonzero 𝐸𝑧 therefore requires

𝑁 2 = 𝜀 ∥ .

This is the ordinary mode. Its electric field is parallel to 𝑩0 but transverse to 𝒌, so the Lorentz force does not alter this component.

Step: Factor the extraordinary block

The determinant of the 𝑥−𝑦 block is

𝜀 ⟂ ( 𝜀 ⟂ − 𝑁 2 ) − 𝜀 × 2 = 0 .

Solving for the index gives

𝑁 𝑋 2 = 𝜀 ⟂ 2 − 𝜀 × 2 𝜀 ⟂ .

For one electron species, define

𝜀 ⟂ = 1 − 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 2 𝜔 2 − 𝜔 𝑐 , 𝑒 2

and

𝜀 × = − 𝜔 𝑐 , 𝑒 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 2 𝜔 ( 𝜔 2 − 𝜔 𝑐 , 𝑒 2 ) .

Step: Expose the upper-hybrid denominator

Let 𝑑=𝜔2−𝜔𝑐,𝑒2 and 𝑝=𝜔𝑝,𝑒2. Then

𝑁 𝑋 2 = ( 𝑑 − 𝑝 ) 2 − ( 𝜔 𝑐 , 𝑒 2 𝑝 2 𝜔 2 ) 𝑑 ( 𝑑 − 𝑝 ) .

Multiplying the two circular factors and simplifying yields

𝑁 𝑋 2 = 1 − 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 2 ( 𝜔 2 − 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 2 ) 𝜔 2 ( 𝜔 2 − 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 2 − 𝜔 𝑐 , 𝑒 2 ) .

The denominator vanishes at

𝜔 UH 2 = 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 2 + 𝜔 𝑐 , 𝑒 2 ,

which is the upper-hybrid resonance.

Step: Read the extraordinary polarization

The first row of the transverse block is

𝜀 ⟂ 𝐸 𝑥 − 𝑖 𝜀 × 𝐸 𝑦 = 0 .

Hence

𝐸 𝑥 𝐸 𝑦 = 𝑖 𝜀 × 𝜀 ⟂ ,

which is generally elliptical rather than purely linear polarization.

Knowledge check

  1. Why is the ordinary mode unaffected by the magnetic field in perpendicular propagation?
    Answer Its electric field is parallel to 𝑩 0 , so the perturbed velocity is parallel to the field and the magnetic part of the Lorentz force vanishes.
  2. What distinguishes the extraordinary mode from the ordinary mode?
    Answer Its electric field lies in the plane perpendicular to 𝑩 0 , with components both along and across 𝒌 . Its index contains both circular factors and its polarization is generally elliptical.
  3. What is the upper-hybrid frequency in the cold fixed-ion model?
    Answer It is 𝜔 UH = 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 2 + 𝜔 𝑐 , 𝑒 2 . It marks the zero of the extraordinary-mode denominator and the associated cold resonance.
  4. How can a branch be evanescent even when the plasma is collisionless?
    Answer A collisionless response can still give 𝑁 2 < 0 . Then 𝑘 is imaginary and the spatial solution decays rather than propagates; this is a stopband, not collisional damping.

13.4 Oblique propagation and mode coupling

At an arbitrary angle the clean parallel and perpendicular factorizations no longer apply. The wave vector has components both along and across the magnetic field, so the two electromagnetic branches are generally elliptically polarized and coupled through the full three-component wave matrix.

  • set up the oblique coordinate system and its angle convention
  • write the full cold magnetized wave matrix
  • identify the two electromagnetic roots of the Appleton–Hartree form
  • recover the parallel and perpendicular limits from the oblique model
Unit ledger The propagation angle 𝜃 is dimensionless. Use 𝑁=𝑘𝑐/𝜔, 𝑋𝜔=𝜔𝑝,𝑒2/𝜔2, and 𝑌𝜔=𝜔𝑐,𝑒/𝜔, all dimensionless. The dimensional wave number 𝑘 is in m−1 and frequency 𝜔 in s−1.

Here 𝑆, 𝐷, and 𝑃 are dimensionless dielectric abbreviations; 𝐷 is not a diffusion coefficient and 𝑃 is not pressure. The parameters 𝑋𝜔 and 𝑌𝜔 use the wave frequency as their reference, whereas the earlier 𝜔̂ and 𝑌 use the plasma frequency. Their relations are 𝑋𝜔=1/𝜔̂2 and 𝑌𝜔=𝑌/𝜔̂.

The two roots give possible wave numbers at a specified frequency and angle. For each root, the matrix also determines the relative electric-field components. Following those components is necessary to identify a physical branch.

Derivation: oblique matrix and limiting geometry

Step: Insert the oblique wave vector

Use

𝒌 = 𝑘 ( sin 𝜃 𝒆 𝑥 + cos 𝜃 𝒆 𝑧 )

in

After division by 𝜔2/𝑐2, the geometric term contributes

− 𝑁 2 cos 2 𝜃 𝐸 𝑥 + 𝑁 2 sin 𝜃 cos 𝜃 𝐸 𝑧

to the 𝑥 component, −𝑁2𝐸𝑦 to the 𝑦 component, and

𝑁 2 sin 𝜃 cos 𝜃 𝐸 𝑥 − 𝑁 2 sin 2 𝜃 𝐸 𝑧

to the 𝑧 component. Adding the dielectric tensor gives the displayed oblique matrix.

Set 𝑍=𝑁2, 𝑎=sin𝜃, and 𝑏=cos𝜃. Expanding the determinant gives

det ( 𝑀 ) = ( 𝑃 − 𝑍 𝑎 2 ) ( ( 𝑆 − 𝑍 𝑏 2 ) ( 𝑆 − 𝑍 ) − 𝐷 2 ) − 𝑍 2 𝑎 2 𝑏 2 ( 𝑆 − 𝑍 ) .

Step: Check the parallel and perpendicular endpoints

For 𝜃=0, the matrix separates into the longitudinal factor 𝑃 and circular transverse factors 𝑆±𝐷. For 𝜃=𝜋/2, the 𝑧 component decouples as the ordinary mode and the 𝑥−𝑦 block produces the extraordinary mode. Without its endpoint polarization, an algebraic root can be assigned to the wrong physical branch.

Knowledge check

  1. Why can the oblique wave matrix not be reduced to the parallel transverse block?
    Answer Both 𝑘 ∥ and 𝑘 ⟂ are nonzero. The geometric part of Maxwell’s equation then couples 𝐸 𝑥 and 𝐸 𝑧 in addition to the dielectric coupling of 𝐸 𝑥 and 𝐸 𝑦 .
  2. How many electromagnetic refractive-index roots does the cold oblique determinant provide?
    Answer The determinant is quadratic in 𝑁 2 , so it provides two electromagnetic roots. Their polarization vectors, not only their scalar indices, identify how they connect to the principal modes.
  3. What happens to the oblique roots as the angle approaches zero?
    Answer The wave vector becomes parallel to the magnetic field, the longitudinal factor separates, and the two electromagnetic roots approach the circularly polarized parallel branches.
  4. Why should a mode label be accompanied by its propagation angle?
    Answer The same branch can change from mostly transverse to partly longitudinal as the angle changes. The polarization and cutoff or resonance locations are angle-dependent even when some principal cutoffs are shared.

13.5 Cutoffs, resonances, and controlled limits

A cutoff, a resonance, an evanescent interval, and a high-frequency vacuum limit are different statements about the same refractive-index equation. Each holds only within the model that produced the diagram.

Learning objectives

  • distinguish 𝑁=0 cutoffs from divergent resonances
  • classify propagation using the sign of 𝑁2
  • recover the unmagnetized and vacuum limits
  • state when warm, collisional, or kinetic corrections are required
Unit ledger The normalized frequency 𝜔̂=𝜔/𝜔𝑝,𝑒 and magnetization 𝑌=𝜔𝑐,𝑒/𝜔𝑝,𝑒 are dimensionless. Define the normalized wave number 𝐾=𝑘𝑐/𝜔𝑝,𝑒=𝜔̂𝑁. The refractive index 𝑁 and angle 𝜃 are dimensionless; dimensional 𝑘 is in m−1, 𝜔 in s−1, and wavelengths in m.
Derivation: limiting-case checks

Step: Remove the magnetic field

Set 𝑌=0 in the circular response. Both eigenvalues become

𝑁 2 = 1 − 1 𝜔 ̂ 2 ,

so multiplying by 𝜔̂2 and using 𝐾=𝜔̂𝑁 gives the cold unmagnetized electromagnetic branch

𝜔 ̂ 2 = 1 + 𝐾 2 .

The longitudinal branch remains 𝜔̂=1 in the cold fixed-ion limit, so its zero group velocity is recovered as well.

Step: Check high-frequency, cutoff, and resonance limits

As 𝜔̂→∞, every susceptibility term scales as 𝜔̂−2 or faster away from a resonance. Therefore

𝑁 2 → 1 , 𝜔 → 𝑐 𝑘 .

The plasma becomes transparent. A cutoff is found from 𝑁2=0 and is a finite-frequency, zero-wave-number endpoint. At a resonance, a denominator such as 𝜔(𝜔−𝜔𝑐,𝑒) or 𝜔2−𝜔UH2 vanishes. Then 𝑁2 becomes large and the wave length becomes small. The cold response is singular because it has no thermal spread, collisions, finite orbit width, or nonlinear saturation.

Step: Classify propagation intervals

For the extraordinary perpendicular branch, the two circular cutoffs and the upper-hybrid resonance divide the frequency axis into propagation and stopband intervals. Determine the correct interval by evaluating the sign of 𝑁𝑋2 between adjacent landmarks. If density or magnetic field varies along a path, a local cutoff can reflect or mode-convert a wave. Approximating the wave locally by a plane wave additionally requires the background scale length to be long compared with the local wavelength; this condition fails near a cutoff where the wavelength grows without bound.

Step: Return to the physical ordering

If 𝑘𝜆𝐷 is not small, pressure and kinetic dispersion matter. If 𝜔 is comparable to a collision frequency, the dielectric coefficients become complex. If 𝜔−𝑘𝑣∥ is near zero for a significant particle population, kinetic susceptibility and resonant damping or growth replace the cold-fluid response.

Knowledge check

  1. What equation identifies a cutoff?
    Answer A cutoff is identified by 𝑁 2 = 0 . The wave number vanishes at the cutoff, unlike a resonance where the refractive index becomes singular.
  2. What is the vacuum limit of the cold magnetized response?
    Answer For frequencies much larger than the plasma and cyclotron frequencies, the dielectric response approaches the identity, 𝑁 2 approaches one, and the dispersion relation approaches 𝜔 = 𝑐 𝑘 .
  3. Why can the sign of 𝑁 2 be used to classify propagation?
    Answer For a real prescribed frequency and a homogeneous lossless cold model, 𝑁 2 > 0 gives real 𝑘 and oscillatory spatial dependence, while 𝑁 2 < 0 gives imaginary 𝑘 and exponential spatial dependence.
  4. Which corrections should be considered near a cold-plasma resonance?
    Answer Finite temperature, collisions, particle-orbit effects, kinetic resonances, boundaries, and nonlinear effects can all regularize or replace the singular cold response. The relevant correction is selected by the ordering of the physical scales.
