10. Collisions and plasma conductivity

Gradients and external forces drive net transport; collisions set how quickly momentum relaxes. Inhomogeneity produces diffusion, while an external force produces mobility and electrical current. Collisions alone create no net flux in a homogeneous equilibrium. Resistivity and conductivity models are built on the collision frequencies.

10.1 Collision bookkeeping and transport mechanisms

What does it mean to say that a particle collides? The useful answer is a rate, a mean free path, and a momentum-transfer rule. A cross section is an effective area; it may depend strongly on relative speed and scattering angle.

Learning objectives

  • distinguish collision frequency, collision time, cross section, and mean free path
  • derive the collision rate from a path-length probability
  • identify diffusion and force-driven mobility as distinct transport routes
  • state the Knudsen ordering that separates fluid-like from collisionless motion
Unit ledger A cross section 𝜎𝑎𝑏 is in m2, mean free path is in m, and collision frequency 𝜈𝑎𝑏 is in s−1. A normalized collision count such as 𝜈𝜏 and a Knudsen number are dimensionless.

With a total scattering cross section, 𝜏𝑎𝑏 is a mean waiting time between encounters. With a momentum-transfer cross section, it is a transport relaxation time and 𝜆𝑎𝑏 is a relaxation length; many small-angle encounters can occur before directed momentum relaxes.

A spatial gradient produces a diffusive particle flux, schematically 𝑮diff=−𝐷𝛁(𝑛), while an external force produces a drift velocity, schematically 𝒖mob=𝜇mob𝑭. The coefficient 𝜇mob in this force-based definition is mobility, with units skg−1; it is not the magnetic moment of Chapter 5. An electric-field mobility instead relates velocity directly to the electric field and includes the charge factor from the force.

Knowledge check

  1. What is the difference between collision time and collision frequency?
    Answer They are reciprocals under the same averaging convention: 𝜏 = 1 / 𝜈 . For a total event cross section, these are the encounter rate and mean waiting time. For a momentum-transfer cross section, they describe directed-momentum relaxation, which can require many small-angle encounters.
  2. What are the units of a collision cross section and mean free path?
    Answer A cross section is an area in m 2 , and a mean free path is a length in m . The collision frequency is in s − 1 .
  3. Which dimensionless parameter compares a mean free path with a device length?
    Answer The Knudsen number 𝐾 n = 𝜆 / 𝐿 does so. Small 𝐾 n supports a local collisional fluid model, whereas large 𝐾 n signals kinetic or ballistic transport.
  4. What distinguishes diffusion from force-driven mobility?
    Answer Diffusion is driven by a spatial gradient and has the schematic flux − 𝐷 𝛁 ( 𝑛 ) . Mobility is driven by an external force and produces a drift proportional to that force. Both coefficients depend on collisions.

10.2 Weakly ionized plasmas: neutral collisions

How does a neutral background impede charged-particle motion? Neutral atoms and molecules act as compact scattering centers. For a weakly ionized plasma, the neutral density is usually much larger than the charged density, so electron–neutral and ion–neutral collisions can dominate even though the plasma remains electrically active.

Learning objectives

  • derive the neutral-collision frequency and mean free path
  • distinguish total scattering from momentum-transfer cross section
  • write the neutral drag force for stationary and moving neutrals
  • explain why momentum and energy relaxation need not have the same rate

The momentum-transfer cross section is the angle-weighted quantity:

𝜎 mt ( 𝑣 ) = ∫ ( 1 − cos 𝜃 sc ) d 𝜎 d Ω d Ω

Here 𝜃sc is the deflection angle and dΩ is a solid-angle element; neither angle carries a dimensional unit. The differential cross section d𝜎/(dΩ) gives scattering area per unit solid angle. The factor 1−cos𝜃sc weights each direction by its loss of forward momentum. A scattering process dominated by small deflections can therefore have a large total cross section but a small momentum-transfer cross section.

Step: Count momentum transfer

A collision changes the average directed momentum by an amount that scales as

Δ 𝒑 𝑎 scales as 𝑚 𝑎 ( 𝒖 𝑎 − 𝒖 𝑛 ) .

During d𝑡, the number of encounters per particle is 𝜈𝑎𝑛d𝑡. Multiplying the momentum change by the charged-particle density gives the force-density scaling

Step: Check the stationary-neutral limit

The force is restorative: it vanishes when the charged species and neutral background share a velocity. For stationary neutrals,

𝑹 𝑎 𝑛 = − 𝑚 𝑎 𝑛 𝑎 𝜈 𝑎 𝑛 𝒖 𝑎 .

The rate is a momentum-transfer rate, not necessarily the frequency of every microscopic encounter.

Step: Separate momentum and energy relaxation

Energy transfer has an additional mass-ratio dependence. An electron can reverse direction in an elastic encounter with a heavy neutral while transferring only a small fraction of its kinetic energy. Excitation and ionization are inelastic energy-loss channels; they belong in an energy equation.

Knowledge check

  1. Why is a momentum-transfer cross section more useful than a total cross section for conductivity?
    Answer Conductivity responds to directed momentum loss. The factor 1 − cos 𝜃 sc weights each event by its momentum change, so small-angle scattering is not counted as a full reversal.
  2. How does the neutral density enter the collision frequency?
    Answer It enters linearly: 𝜈 en = 𝑛 n ⟨ 𝜎 mt,en 𝑣 e ⟩ . Doubling the target density doubles the rate when the cross section and velocity distribution are unchanged.
  3. What is the drag force when the neutral background moves?
    Answer Use the relative velocity: 𝑹 en = − 𝑚 e 𝑛 e 𝜈 en ( 𝒖 e − 𝒖 n ) . It vanishes when the two populations share a velocity.
  4. Why can momentum relaxation be faster than energy relaxation in electron–neutral collisions?
    Answer A heavy neutral can redirect an electron efficiently while taking only a small fraction of its kinetic energy. Directional momentum loss and energy exchange therefore have different mass-ratio scalings.

10.3 Fully ionized plasmas: Coulomb collisions

Why is a Coulomb collision not characterized by one hard-sphere radius? The interaction is long range, so most encounters produce small deflections and a few produce large deflections. A controlled collision model must set a lower strong-scattering scale and an upper shielding scale before it can sum the cumulative angular diffusion.

Learning objectives

  • define the ninety-degree impact parameter and large-angle collision rate
  • show why cumulative small-angle scattering produces a logarithm
  • use Debye shielding as the upper impact-parameter cutoff
  • state the weak-coupling condition behind a Coulomb collision frequency
Unit ledger The impact parameter 𝑏90 and the reduced de Broglie wavelength ƛ are in m; the Sommerfeld parameter 𝜂 is dimensionless. The Coulomb cutoff logarithm lnΛcut and plasma parameter Λ=𝑛e𝜆𝐷3 are dimensionless, as is lnΛ.

The large-angle estimate is:

These logarithms are not numerically equal. With the electron Debye length 𝜆𝐷=𝜀0𝑘𝐵𝑇e/(𝑛e𝑒2) and the Maxwellian mean speed used here, Λcut=32Λ, so lnΛcut=lnΛ+ln(32). Treating the additive constant as negligible is only a leading-log approximation when lnΛ is large compared with it. The rate and numerical tasks below retain lnΛ and the cited approximate prefactor; the impact-parameter integral uses lnΛcut.

The plasma parameter Λ also differs from the Debye-sphere particle count used in Chapter 4: 𝑁𝐷=(4𝜋/3)Λ. Both are dimensionless and express the many-particle screening condition. Keeping these definitions separate avoids introducing an unintended factor into a quoted rate.

  1. Why is the ninety-degree cross section not the complete Coulomb collision rate?
    Answer Distant encounters are much more numerous. Each gives a small deflection, but their squared kicks add over impact parameters and produce the cutoff logarithm ln Λ cut . The cited rate estimate instead uses the plasma-parameter logarithm ln Λ .

  3. What sets the upper impact-parameter cutoff in a plasma?
    Answer Collective Debye shielding sets 𝑏 max ≈ 𝜆 𝐷 . Encounters at larger scales are screened by the surrounding plasma and cannot be added as independent unscreened Coulomb kicks.
  4. What condition makes the weakly coupled Coulomb model self-consistent?
    Answer The plasma parameter must be large: Λ = 𝑛 e 𝜆 𝐷 3 ≫ 1 . It implies many particles occupy a Debye sphere and supports a statistical, weak-coupling description.

10.4 Specific resistivity and Spitzer scaling

How does collisional drag become a resistivity? The current is a measure of relative charge-carrier motion, while drag removes that relative momentum. In a steady scalar response, the electric force balances that drag. Expressing the drag in terms of current then gives the resistivity; the collision model supplies the momentum-transfer frequency.

  1. What physical quantity is divided by the charge density to obtain resistivity?
    Answer The collisional drag force density is divided by the charge density scale and expressed per current. This gives 𝜂 = 𝑚 e 𝜈 / ( 𝑛 e 𝑒 2 ) in the scalar model.
  2. How are resistivity and conductivity related?
    Answer They are reciprocals in the scalar model: 𝜎 = 1 / 𝜂 . Resistivity has units Ω m and conductivity has units S m − 1 when 𝑬 = 𝜂 𝒋 .
  3. What is the dominant temperature scaling of Spitzer resistivity?
    Answer At fixed Coulomb logarithm, 𝜂 Sp ∝ 𝑇 e − 3 / 2 . The logarithm varies only weakly with density and temperature in a weakly coupled plasma.
  4. Why must the collision channel be stated before quoting a resistivity?
    Answer Because 𝜈 may be electron–neutral, electron–ion, or a sum of momentum-transfer channels. Different target populations and cross sections produce different drag and therefore different resistivity.

10.5 Plasma conductivity: DC, AC, and ion motion

How does a magnetic field change the relation between electric field and current? It makes the response anisotropic. Current parallel to the field is not gyroscopically deflected, while perpendicular current is split into a dissipative Pedersen part and a nondissipative Hall part. At finite frequency, the response is generally complex: current can have both an amplitude change and a phase shift relative to the applied field.

Learning objectives

  • derive the unmagnetized DC conductivity from the linear momentum equation
  • construct the magnetized conductivity tensor and identify its three components
  • extend the tensor to harmonic AC response
  • include ion motion and identify the limits of an electron-only model
Unit ledger The conductivity tensor entries are in Sm−1.

Direct-current (DC) response uses a steady field; alternating-current (AC) response uses a field oscillating at angular frequency 𝜔. With the stated complex convention, the physical field and current are the real parts of their harmonic representations. The factor −𝑖𝜔 replaces a time derivative.

The linear electron momentum equation becomes

𝑚e(𝜈e−𝑖𝜔)𝒖e=𝑞e(𝑬+𝒖e×𝑩0).

An electron-only model is appropriate only when ions are fixed on the frequency and collision scales; at low frequency, ion motion can change every tensor entry.

Knowledge check

  1. Why is the conductivity tensor anisotropic in a magnetic field?
    Answer Gyromotion deflects a perpendicular drift but does not deflect motion parallel to 𝑩 0 . The electric-field direction therefore no longer determines the current direction by a scalar coefficient.
  2. What is the physical distinction between Pedersen and Hall current?
    Answer Pedersen current follows the perpendicular electric field and dissipates energy through collisions. Hall current is perpendicular to both the perpendicular electric field and 𝑩 0 and reverses with the signed charge convention.
  3. How is DC conductivity generalized to harmonic AC response?
    Answer For fields proportional to exp ( − 𝑖 𝜔 𝑡 ) , replace 𝜈 by 𝑎 = 𝜈 − 𝑖 𝜔 in the coupled momentum equations. The resulting conductivities are complex and contain collisional drag and inertia.
  4. When must ion motion be included in the conductivity?
    Answer Include it when the frequency is low enough, or the collision and magnetization ordering is such, that ions acquire a significant drift. Then use the additive species current 𝒋 = ∑ 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝑞 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 rather than an electron-only model.
