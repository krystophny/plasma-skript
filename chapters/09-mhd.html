9. Single-fluid theory and magnetohydrodynamics

Chapter 8 combined species equations into total-fluid balances. To obtain magnetohydrodynamics (MHD), we also need an equation for the electric field and assumptions that close pressure and heat transport. The resulting model evolves the bulk flow together with the magnetic field. Its ideal and resistive forms apply when charge separation and the omitted species effects are small on the length and time scales of interest.

9.1 Single-fluid variables and conservation laws

What does a one-fluid variable mean when electrons and ions still move differently? It is a carefully chosen sum. The mass-weighted velocity carries total momentum, while charge-weighted velocity differences still define the current.

Learning objectives

  • define the mass, charge, current, pressure, and velocity variables of a single-fluid model
  • derive total mass and charge conservation from species balances
  • identify the relative-flow stress retained in the summed momentum flux
  • separate exact sums from quasi-neutral and one-fluid approximations

For the hydrogen plasma used here, 𝑞𝑒=−𝑒, 𝑞𝑖=+𝑒, and the mass velocity is approximately the ion velocity only when 𝑚𝑖≫𝑚𝑒 and the current correction is small. Quasi-neutrality is the ordering 𝜌𝑞≈0; it does not remove 𝒋.

The exact summed mass balance is

𝜕 𝜌 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝛁 · ( 𝜌 𝒖 ) = 0

Charge-weighting the species continuity equations gives

𝜕 𝜌 𝑞 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝛁 · ( 𝒋 ) = 0

The summed momentum balance from the multiple-fluid chapter is

𝜕 𝜌 𝒖 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝛁 · ( 𝜌 𝒖 𝒖 + 𝑷 ) = 𝜌 𝑞 𝑬 + 𝒋 × 𝑩 + ∑ 𝑠 𝑹 𝑠

For isolated elastic interspecies collisions, ∑𝑠𝑹𝑠=𝟎. In a quasi-neutral, nonrelativistic ordering the electric force density 𝜌𝑞𝑬 is subleading in the bulk momentum equation, but the magnetic force density remains through the total current.

Derivation: sum species moments without losing relative flow

Step: Sum the mass balances

Start with

𝜕 𝑛 𝑠 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝛁 · ( 𝑛 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 ) = 0 .

Multiply by the constant mass 𝑚𝑠 and sum:

∑ 𝑠 𝑚 𝑠 𝜕 𝑛 𝑠 𝜕 𝑡 + ∑ 𝑠 𝑚 𝑠 𝛁 · ( 𝑛 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 ) = 0 .

With 𝜌=∑𝑠𝜌𝑠 and 𝜌𝒖=∑𝑠𝜌𝑠𝒖𝑠, this becomes the total mass balance.

Step: Sum the charge balances

Multiply the species equations by 𝑞𝑠 instead. The two terms become

𝜕 ∑ 𝑠 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝜕 𝑡 = 𝜕 𝜌 𝑞 𝜕 𝑡 , 𝛁 · ( ∑ 𝑠 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 ) = 𝛁 · ( 𝒋 ) .

This gives charge conservation without using a quasi-neutral approximation.

Step: Decompose the summed momentum flux

Add the conservative species momentum equations. Write 𝒖𝑠=𝒖+𝑽𝑠. Since

∑ 𝑠 𝜌 𝑠 𝑽 𝑠 = 𝟎 ,

the flux decomposes as

∑ 𝑠 𝜌 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 = 𝜌 𝒖 𝒖 + ∑ 𝑠 𝜌 𝑠 𝑽 𝑠 𝑽 𝑠 .

The last dyadic is the relative-flow stress and remains part of the total pressure tensor.

Step: Sum the forces

The electromagnetic sums are

∑ 𝑠 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 𝑬 = 𝜌 𝑞 𝑬 , ∑ 𝑠 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 ( 𝒖 𝑠 × 𝑩 ) = 𝒋 × 𝑩 .

Internal collision sources cancel only when their species sum is zero. These identities yield the displayed total momentum equation.

    Answer The velocity difference can remain nonzero. For hydrogen, 𝒋 = 𝑒 𝑛 𝑖 𝒖 𝑖 − 𝑒 𝑛 𝑒 𝒖 𝑒 can be finite even when 𝜌 𝑞 = 𝑒 ( 𝑛 𝑖 − 𝑛 𝑒 ) ≈ 0 .

9.2 Generalized Ohm’s law

How does the current determine the electric field seen by the bulk plasma? The answer comes from the electron momentum equation. Subtracting or rearranging species momentum does not create an arbitrary constitutive law: the Hall, pressure, resistive, and electron-inertia terms each have a definite origin.

Learning objectives

  • derive the generalized Ohm-law balance from the electron equation
  • identify the Hall, electron-pressure, resistive, and electron-inertia terms
  • relate collisional drag to the scalar resistivity and conductivity
  • state which ordering produces the ideal electric-field constraint
Unit ledger A scalar resistivity defined by 𝑬=𝜂𝒋 has units Ωm; the conductivity 𝜎=1/𝜂 is in Sm−1. The electron pressure force per charge density is in Vm−1. The coefficient 𝑚𝑒/(𝑒2𝑛) multiplying a current time derivative has the units needed to produce an electric field.

The electron momentum equation is

𝑚 𝑒 𝑛 ( 𝜕 𝒖 𝑒 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝒖 𝑒 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝒖 𝑒 ) ) = − 𝑒 𝑛 ( 𝑬 + 𝒖 𝑒 × 𝑩 ) − 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑒 ) + 𝑹 𝑒

Solving it for the field in the electron frame and replacing 𝒖𝑒 by 𝒖−𝒋/(𝑒𝑛) gives the ordered generalized Ohm law

𝑬 + 𝒖 × 𝑩 = 𝒋 × 𝑩 𝑒 𝑛 − 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑒 ) 𝑒 𝑛 + 𝜂 𝒋 + 𝑚 𝑒 𝑒 2 𝑛 𝜕 𝒋 𝜕 𝑡

The four right-hand terms are, respectively, the Hall response, the electron-pressure or ambipolar response, collisional resistivity, and the leading electron-inertia response in a slowly varying bulk frame.

The left side is the electric field seen by the moving bulk fluid to nonrelativistic order. The Hall term appears because electrons carry the current relative to that fluid; the pressure term supplies the field needed to balance electron pressure. Resistive drag opposes relative motion, while electron inertia matters when the current changes rapidly.

For a linear electron–ion drag law, the force on electrons is

𝑹 𝑒 = 𝑚 𝑒 𝑛 𝜈 𝑒 𝑖 ( 𝒖 𝑖 − 𝒖 𝑒 ) = 𝑚 𝑒 𝜈 𝑒 𝑖 𝒋 𝑒

Therefore

𝜂 = 𝑚 𝑒 𝜈 𝑒 𝑖 𝑛 𝑒 2 , 𝜎 = 1 𝜂 = 𝑛 𝑒 2 𝑚 𝑒 𝜈 𝑒 𝑖

Here 𝜈𝑒𝑖 is the electron–ion momentum-transfer frequency, and 𝜎 is the scalar conductivity in Sm−1. The frequency itself depends on the collision model and plasma state; the later collisions chapter supplies that kinetic input. Here, resistivity enters as drag per unit current.

Derivation: locate every term in generalized Ohm’s law

Step: Isolate the electric field

Start with the electron material momentum equation and move the electric force to the left:

𝑬 + 𝒖 𝑒 × 𝑩 = − 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑒 ) 𝑒 𝑛 + 𝑹 𝑒 𝑒 𝑛 − 𝑚 𝑒 ( 𝜕 𝒖 𝑒 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝒖 𝑒 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝒖 𝑒 ) ) 𝑒 .

Step: Expose the Hall term

Since

𝒖 − 𝒖 𝑒 ≈ 𝒋 𝑒 𝑛 ,

adding the difference between bulk and electron magnetic advection gives

𝑬 + 𝒖 × 𝑩 = − 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑒 ) 𝑒 𝑛 + 𝑹 𝑒 𝑒 𝑛 − 𝑚 𝑒 ( 𝜕 𝒖 𝑒 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝒖 𝑒 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝒖 𝑒 ) ) 𝑒 + 𝒋 × 𝑩 𝑒 𝑛 .

The displayed Hall contribution is the magnetic force expressed through the electron–bulk relative velocity.

Step: Model pressure and resistive drag

For scalar electron pressure, retain −𝛁(𝑝𝑒)/(𝑒𝑛) as one term; splitting it further is a separate ordering choice. Model interspecies drag as

𝑹 𝑒 = 𝑚 𝑒 𝑛 𝜈 𝑒 𝑖 ( 𝒖 𝑖 − 𝒖 𝑒 ) .

Since 𝒖𝑖−𝒖𝑒=𝒋/(𝑒𝑛),

𝑹 𝑒 𝑒 𝑛 = 𝑚 𝑒 𝜈 𝑒 𝑖 𝒋 𝑛 𝑒 2 = 𝜂 𝒋 .

Thus

𝜂 = 𝑚 𝑒 𝜈 𝑒 𝑖 𝑛 𝑒 2 , 𝜎 = 1 𝜂 .

Step: Retain electron inertia when needed

Write

𝒖 𝑒 ≈ 𝒖 − 𝒋 𝑒 𝑛 .

In the slowly varying-density and slow-bulk-inertia ordering, the current-dependent part of the electron inertia is

𝑚 𝑒 𝜕 𝒋 𝜕 𝑡 𝑒 2 𝑛 .

If current advection or density variation is not small, replace this approximation by the full electron-inertia operator; the displayed generalized law is then insufficient.

    Answer The electron momentum equation is the direct source. Solving it for the electric field and replacing the electron–bulk velocity difference with 𝒋 / ( 𝑒 𝑛 ) produces the Hall and current terms.
  2. What physical process gives the scalar resistivity?
    Answer

    Electron–ion collisional drag gives

    𝑹 𝑒 = 𝑚 𝑒 𝜈 𝑒 𝑖 𝒋 𝑒 ,

    hence 𝜂=𝑚𝑒𝜈𝑒𝑖/(𝑛𝑒2). More frequent momentum transfer means larger resistivity and smaller conductivity.

  3. When is the Hall term important?
    Answer It matters when 𝒋 × 𝑩 / ( 𝑒 𝑛 ) is not small compared with the bulk electric field. Relative to magnetic advection, its magnitude is set by | 𝒋 ⟂ | / ( 𝑒 𝑛 | 𝒖 ⟂ | ) : the common factor 𝐵 cancels. Increasing 𝐵 alone does not increase this ratio. A scale ordering must justify neglecting the Hall term.

    Answer The condition is 𝑬 + 𝒖 × 𝑩 = 𝟎 . It is a reduced generalized Ohm law and therefore requires all retained correction terms to be small.

9.3 Linearized and simplified MHD equations

Which finite set of equations is actually called MHD? After the current response has been ordered, the plasma can be described by mass density, bulk velocity, pressure, and magnetic field. Linearization around a uniform equilibrium then exposes the small-amplitude system that leads to the wave chapters.

Learning objectives

  • state the nonlinear ideal-MHD conservation laws and closures
  • linearize mass, momentum, induction, and pressure equations about a uniform equilibrium
  • define the sound-speed and magnetic-field constraints in the linear system
  • distinguish a linear perturbation model from the underlying nonlinear equations
Unit ledger The adiabatic sound speed 𝑐𝑠=𝛾𝑝0/𝜌0 is in ms−1. Perturbation symbols such as 𝛿𝜌/𝜌0, 𝛿𝑝/𝑝0, and 𝛿𝑩/𝐵0 are dimensionless ratios.

The nonlinear ideal-MHD equations are

𝜕 𝜌 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝛁 · ( 𝜌 𝒖 ) = 0 𝜌 ( 𝜕 𝒖 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝒖 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝒖 ) ) = − 𝛁 ( 𝑝 ) + 𝛁 × ( 𝑩 ) × 𝑩 𝜇 0 𝜕 𝑩 𝜕 𝑡 = 𝛁 × ( 𝒖 × 𝑩 ) , 𝛁 · ( 𝑩 ) = 0 𝜕 𝑝 𝜌 − 𝛾 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝒖 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝜌 − 𝛾 ) = 0

The magnetic force has the coefficient 1/𝜇0 because the displacement current 𝜇0𝜀0𝜕𝑬/𝜕𝑡 has been neglected in Ampere’s law (nonrelativistic flows, 𝑢2≪𝑐2, and slow time scales):

𝒋 = 𝛁 × ( 𝑩 ) 𝜇 0

The electric field is recovered from the ideal constraint, not evolved as an independent MHD variable.

Let 𝜌=𝜌0+𝛿𝜌, 𝑝=𝑝0+𝛿𝑝, 𝒖=𝛿𝒖, and 𝑩=𝑩0+𝛿𝑩, where perturbations are first order. For uniform equilibrium, the linearized equations are

𝜕 𝛿 𝜌 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝜌 0 𝛁 · ( 𝛿 𝒖 ) = 0 𝜌 0 𝜕 𝛿 𝒖 𝜕 𝑡 = − 𝛁 ( 𝛿 𝑝 ) + 𝛁 × ( 𝛿 𝑩 ) × 𝑩 0 𝜇 0 𝜕 𝛿 𝑩 𝜕 𝑡 = 𝛁 × ( 𝛿 𝒖 × 𝑩 0 ) , 𝛁 · ( 𝛿 𝑩 ) = 0 𝛿 𝑝 = 𝑐 𝑠 2 𝛿 𝜌 , 𝑐 𝑠 2 = 𝛾 𝑝 0 𝜌 0

Here the subscript in 𝑐𝑠 labels sound speed, not a particle species. Linearization keeps the response proportional to one perturbation and discards products of perturbations. The pressure relation also uses the initial entropy assumption; it does not follow from small amplitude alone.

Derivation: linearize the ideal-MHD system

Step: Linearize continuity

Insert

𝜌 = 𝜌 0 + 𝛿 𝜌 , 𝒖 = 𝛿 𝒖

into

𝜕 𝜌 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝛁 · ( 𝜌 𝒖 ) = 0 .

Since 𝜌0 is constant and products of perturbations are second order,

𝜕 𝛿 𝜌 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝜌 0 𝛁 · ( 𝛿 𝒖 ) = 0 .

Step: Linearize momentum

The equilibrium has 𝒖0=𝟎 and no pressure gradient. The inertial term becomes

𝜌 ( 𝜕 𝒖 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝒖 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝒖 ) ) = 𝜌 0 𝜕 𝛿 𝒖 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝑂 ( 𝛿 2 ) .

The pressure force is −𝛁(𝛿𝑝). Expanding the magnetic force and using 𝛁×(𝑩0)=𝟎 leaves

[ 𝛁 × ( 𝛿 𝑩 ) × 𝑩 0 ] 𝜇 0 .

Step: Linearize induction and magnetic solenoidality

Keeping one perturbation and one background field in ideal induction gives

𝜕 𝛿 𝑩 𝜕 𝑡 = 𝛁 × ( 𝛿 𝒖 × 𝑩 0 ) ,

while divergence-free magnetic fields give

𝛁 · ( 𝛿 𝑩 ) = 0 .

Step: Linearize the adiabatic closure

Expand 𝑝𝜌−𝛾 about the static equilibrium. The first-order invariant is proportional to

𝛿 𝑝 𝑝 0 − 𝛾 ( 𝛿 𝜌 𝜌 0 ) .

Its material derivative reduces to a time derivative, so 𝜕𝛿𝑝−𝑐𝑠2𝛿𝜌/𝜕𝑡=0. Initially isentropic perturbations therefore satisfy

𝛿 𝑝 𝑝 0 = 𝛾 ( 𝛿 𝜌 𝜌 0 ) , 𝛿 𝑝 = ( 𝛾 𝑝 0 𝜌 0 ) 𝛿 𝜌 = 𝑐 𝑠 2 𝛿 𝜌 .

Every discarded product contains at least two perturbation factors.

9.4 Frozen-in flux and magnetic diffusion

What does ideal MHD mean geometrically? Its induction equation says that magnetic flux through a surface moving with the fluid is conserved. A finite resistivity adds a competing diffusion process, so the magnetic Reynolds number decides whether field lines are effectively carried by the flow or can slip through it.

Learning objectives

  • derive the induction equation from Faraday’s law and Ohm’s law
  • prove conservation of magnetic flux through a material surface
  • define magnetic diffusivity and magnetic Reynolds number
  • estimate the magnetic-field diffusion timescale and its limiting regime
Unit ledger The magnetic diffusivity 𝐷𝐵=𝜂/𝜇0 is in m2s−1. The magnetic Reynolds number 𝑅𝑚=𝑈𝐿/𝐷𝐵 is dimensionless. The advection time 𝜏𝐴=𝐿/𝑈 and diffusion time 𝜏𝐷=𝐿2/𝐷𝐵 are both in s.

Faraday’s law and generalized Ohm’s law in the resistive MHD limit are

𝛁 × ( 𝑬 ) = − 𝜕 𝑩 𝜕 𝑡 , 𝑬 + 𝒖 × 𝑩 = 𝜂 𝒋

Substitution of reduced Ampere’s law gives the induction equation

𝜕 𝑩 𝜕 𝑡 = 𝛁 × ( 𝒖 × 𝑩 ) + 𝐷 𝐵 ∇ 2 ( 𝑩 ) , 𝐷 𝐵 = 𝜂 𝜇 0

The first term advects and stretches field; the second diffuses it. The dimensionless competition is

𝑅 𝑚 = 𝑈 𝐿 𝐷 𝐵 = 𝜏 𝐷 𝜏 𝐴

For 𝑅𝑚≫1, the flow crosses the scale 𝐿 much sooner than resistivity smooths magnetic structure on that scale. This compares two times; it does not say that diffusion is slow on every observation time. For a characteristic structure of size 𝐿, the finite-resistivity diffusion estimate is

𝜏 𝐷 ≈ 𝐿 2 𝐷 𝐵 = 𝜇 0 𝐿 2 𝜂

In the ideal limit, follow a loop of fluid elements and a surface spanning that loop. The magnetic flux through the moving surface stays constant even as the surface stretches or bends. The local field strength can still change: frozen flux does not mean a static or spatially uniform field.

Derivation: induction equation and frozen magnetic flux

Step: Insert resistivity into Faraday’s law

Begin with

𝑬 + 𝒖 × 𝑩 = 𝜂 𝒋 ,

so

𝑬 = − 𝒖 × 𝑩 + 𝜂 𝒋 .

Faraday’s law becomes

𝜕 𝑩 𝜕 𝑡 = − 𝛁 × ( 𝑬 ) = 𝛁 × ( 𝒖 × 𝑩 ) − 𝜂 𝛁 × ( 𝒋 ) .

Step: Convert the current term to magnetic diffusion

The reduced Ampere law is

𝒋 = 𝛁 × ( 𝑩 ) 𝜇 0 .

For uniform 𝜂,

− 𝜂 𝛁 × ( 𝒋 ) = − 𝜂 𝛁 × ( 𝛁 × ( 𝑩 ) ) 𝜇 0 .

Use

𝛁 × ( 𝛁 × ( 𝑩 ) ) = 𝛁 ( 𝛁 · ( 𝑩 ) ) − ∇ 2 ( 𝑩 ) .

With 𝛁·(𝑩)=0,

− 𝜂 𝛁 × ( 𝒋 ) = 𝜂 ∇ 2 ( 𝑩 ) 𝜇 0 .

Define 𝐷𝐵=𝜂/𝜇0 to obtain the displayed induction equation.

Step: Prove frozen magnetic flux in the ideal limit

Let 𝑆(𝑡) be a surface whose boundary moves with 𝒖 and define

Ψ 𝐵 = ∫ 𝑆 ( 𝑡 ) 𝑩 ⋅ d 𝑺 .

The moving-surface transport theorem gives

d Ψ 𝐵 d 𝑡 = ∫ 𝑆 ( 𝑡 ) [ 𝜕 𝑩 𝜕 𝑡 − 𝛁 × ( 𝒖 × 𝑩 ) ] ⋅ d 𝑺 .

Ideal induction sets the integrand to zero, so dΨ𝐵/d𝑡=0. Stokes’ theorem gives the equivalent moving-loop statement. Thus flux through every material surface is constant.

Step: Compare advection and diffusion

The induction terms scale as

| 𝛁 × ( 𝒖 × 𝑩 ) | ≈ 𝑈 𝐵 𝐿 , | 𝐷 𝐵 ∇ 2 ( 𝑩 ) | ≈ 𝐷 𝐵 𝐵 𝐿 2 .

Their ratio is

𝑈 𝐿 𝐷 𝐵 = 𝑅 𝑚 = 𝐿 2 𝐷 𝐵 𝐿 𝑈 = 𝜏 𝐷 𝜏 𝐴 .

This magnetic Reynolds number compares advection with resistive diffusion.

    Answer The term 𝐷 𝐵 ∇ 2 ( 𝑩 ) represents magnetic diffusion. It is absent in ideal MHD and becomes important when its scale estimate is comparable with the advection term.
  2. What surface is used in the frozen-flux statement?
    Answer It is a material surface whose boundary moves with the bulk velocity 𝒖 . Flux through a fixed laboratory surface is not the same invariant.
  3. How does the magnetic Reynolds number compare the timescales?
    Answer 𝑅 𝑚 = 𝜏 𝐷 / 𝜏 𝐴 = 𝑈 𝐿 / 𝐷 𝐵 . Thus 𝑅 𝑚 ≫ 1 means advection is faster than diffusion, while 𝑅 𝑚 ≪ 1 means diffusion acts before the flow can transport the field across 𝐿 .
  4. What assumption is needed to replace the double curl by a Laplacian?
    Answer Use 𝛁 · ( 𝑩 ) = 0 and uniform resistivity. Then 𝛁 × ( 𝛁 × ( 𝑩 ) ) = − ∇ 2 ( 𝑩 ) , yielding the diffusion term with coefficient 𝐷 𝐵 = 𝜂 / 𝜇 0 .

9.5 Static MHD equilibrium and magnetic force balance

How can a plasma remain static while its pressure varies across the device? In a magnetized equilibrium, the current creates a magnetic force that balances the pressure gradient. The vector identity for this force separates magnetic pressure from field-line tension and makes cylindrical pinch configurations interpretable.

Learning objectives

  • derive the static MHD equilibrium equation from the momentum balance
  • separate magnetic-pressure and magnetic-tension contributions
  • deduce the restrictions on pressure along field lines and current across them
  • explain why the parallel current requires field-line geometry and closure
  • interpret theta-pinch, cylindrical pinch, and plasma-beta examples
Unit ledger Magnetic pressure 𝐵2/(2𝜇0) has the same unit as static pressure, Pa. The plasma beta 𝛽=2𝜇0𝑝/𝐵2 is dimensionless.

The static momentum equation is

𝛁 ( 𝑝 ) = 𝒋 × 𝑩 = 𝛁 × ( 𝑩 ) × 𝑩 𝜇 0

Using the vector identity 𝛁×(𝑩)×𝑩=(𝑩⋅𝛁)𝑩−𝛁(𝐵2/2), this becomes

𝛁 ( 𝑝 + 𝐵 2 2 𝜇 0 ) = 1 𝜇 0 ( 𝑩 ⋅ 𝛁 ) 𝑩

The left side is the gradient of gas plus magnetic pressure. The right side is field-line tension and vanishes for a straight, uniform field.

Dotting the force balance with 𝑩 and with 𝒋 gives

𝑩 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝑝 ) = 0 , 𝒋 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝑝 ) = 0

Thus pressure is constant along each field line, and the current is tangent to pressure surfaces when the scalar-pressure equilibrium is valid. The perpendicular current follows by crossing the equilibrium with 𝑩:

𝒋 ⟂ = 𝑩 × 𝛁 ( 𝑝 ) 𝐵 2

The force balance fixes only this perpendicular component. Write the total current as

𝒋 = 𝑗 ∥ ( 𝑩 𝐵 ) + 𝒋 ⟂

Because reduced Ampere’s law also implies 𝛁·(𝒋)=0, the parallel component obeys the magnetic differential equation

𝑩 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝑗 ∥ 𝐵 ) = − 𝛁 · ( 𝒋 ⟂ )

Thus 𝑗∥ requires field-line geometry and boundary or closure data; it is not determined by the local pressure balance alone.

The ratio of thermal to magnetic pressure is the plasma beta,

𝛽 = 2 𝜇 0 𝑝 𝐵 2

Low 𝛽 means magnetic pressure exceeds thermal pressure; high 𝛽 means the reverse. This local ratio compares pressures, not their gradients. The forces also depend on spatial variation and field-line curvature, so 𝛽 alone does not determine an equilibrium or its stability.

The two radial balances make the distinction quantitative:

For a 𝜃-pinch,

d 𝑝 + 𝐵 𝑧 2 2 𝜇 0 d 𝑟 = 0

For a cylindrical or 𝑧-pinch,

d 𝑝 + 𝐵 𝜃 2 2 𝜇 0 d 𝑟 + 𝐵 𝜃 2 𝜇 0 𝑟 = 0

The second term is the curvature tension of the azimuthal field. Both relations are special reductions of the same vector equilibrium equation; neither replaces the general force balance.

Derivation: magnetic pressure, tension, and pinch balance

Step: Decompose the magnetic force

Begin with the static momentum equation and reduced Ampere’s law:

𝟎 = − 𝛁 ( 𝑝 ) + 𝒋 × 𝑩


𝒋 = 𝛁 × ( 𝑩 ) 𝜇 0 .

Therefore,

𝛁 ( 𝑝 ) = ( 𝛁 × ( 𝑩 ) ) × 𝑩 𝜇 0 .

Use the standard identity

𝛁 ( 𝐵 2 2 ) = ( 𝑩 ⋅ 𝛁 ) 𝑩 + 𝑩 × ( 𝛁 × ( 𝑩 ) ) .

Since 𝑩×𝛁×(𝑩)=−𝛁×(𝑩)×𝑩, rearrangement gives

𝛁 × ( 𝑩 ) × 𝑩 = ( 𝑩 ⋅ 𝛁 ) 𝑩 − 𝛁 ( 𝐵 2 2 ) .

Substitution separates magnetic pressure from field-line tension:

𝛁 ( 𝑝 + 𝐵 2 2 𝜇 0 ) = ( 𝑩 ⋅ 𝛁 ) 𝑩 𝜇 0 .

The gradient on the left describes compression of the field magnitude; the term on the right describes the tension of curved field lines.

Step: Derive the pressure and current constraints

Dot the force balance with 𝑩. The right-hand side vanishes, so

𝑩 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝑝 ) = 0 .

Dot it with 𝒋 instead. Again the right-hand side vanishes:

𝒋 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝑝 ) = 0 .

Next cross the force balance from the left with 𝑩:

𝑩 × 𝛁 ( 𝑝 ) = 𝑩 × ( 𝒋 × 𝑩 ) .

The triple-product identity gives

𝑩 × ( 𝒋 × 𝑩 ) = 𝐵 2 𝒋 − 𝑩 ( 𝑩 ⋅ 𝒋 ) .

Thus the current perpendicular to the field is

𝒋 ⟂ = 𝑩 × 𝛁 ( 𝑝 ) 𝐵 2 .

Decompose the current as

𝒋 = 𝑗 ∥ ( 𝑩 𝐵 ) + 𝒋 ⟂ .

Because reduced Ampere’s law and 𝛁·(𝑩)=0 imply 𝛁·(𝒋)=0, the parallel component obeys

0 = 𝛁 · ( 𝒋 ) = 𝛁 · ( 𝑗 ∥ 𝑩 𝐵 ) + 𝛁 · ( 𝒋 ⟂ ) = 𝑩 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝑗 ∥ 𝐵 ) + 𝛁 · ( 𝒋 ⟂ ) .

A boundary condition or a separate closure is needed to select the parallel-current solution along each field line.

Step: Reduce the equation to a straight-field pinch

For a straight cylindrical field 𝑩=𝐵𝑧(𝑟)𝒆𝑧, reduced Ampere’s law gives

𝒋 = − 1 𝜇 0 d 𝐵 𝑧 d 𝑟 𝒆 𝜃 .

The radial force balance becomes

d 𝑝 d 𝑟 = − 𝐵 𝑧 d 𝐵 𝑧 d 𝑟 𝜇 0 ,

so

d 𝑝 + 𝐵 𝑧 2 2 𝜇 0 d 𝑟 = 0 .

For a 𝜃-pinch with a uniform axial field inside, volume current and magnetic tension can vanish in the interior. The pressure change is then balanced by the magnetic-pressure change across the boundary.

Step: Include field-line curvature in a 𝑧-pinch

For a cylindrical or 𝑧-pinch, take 𝑩=𝐵𝜃(𝑟)𝒆𝜃 and 𝒋=𝑗𝑧(𝑟)𝒆𝑧. Cylindrical Ampere’s law gives

𝑗 𝑧 = 1 𝜇 0 𝑟 d 𝑟 𝐵 𝜃 d 𝑟 .

Since 𝒆𝑧×𝒆𝜃=−𝒆𝑟, the radial balance is

d 𝑝 d 𝑟 = − ( 𝐵 𝜃 𝜇 0 𝑟 ) d 𝑟 𝐵 𝜃 d 𝑟 .

Expanding the derivative yields

d 𝑝 + 𝐵 𝜃 2 2 𝜇 0 d 𝑟 + 𝐵 𝜃 2 𝜇 0 𝑟 = 0 .

The final term is the inward magnetic tension from curved field lines.

Knowledge check

  1. What is the static MHD force-balance equation?
    Answer

    With the stated assumptions,

    𝛁 ( 𝑝 ) = 𝒋 × 𝑩 = ( 𝛁 × ( 𝑩 ) ) × 𝑩 𝜇 0 .
  2. What does the magnetic-pressure/tension decomposition show?
    Answer

    It rewrites the force as

    𝛁 ( 𝑝 + 𝐵 2 2 𝜇 0 ) = ( 𝑩 ⋅ 𝛁 ) 𝑩 𝜇 0 .

    The gradient term is magnetic pressure and the directional derivative is field-line tension.

  3. What is fixed locally about the current, and what determines its parallel component?
    Answer The pressure balance fixes 𝒋 ⟂ = 𝑩 × 𝛁 ( 𝑝 ) / 𝐵 2 . The parallel component is constrained by 𝑩 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝑗 ∥ / 𝐵 ) = − 𝛁 · ( 𝒋 ⟂ ) and therefore requires field-line geometry and boundary or closure data.
  4. What does a large plasma beta indicate?
    Answer A large 𝛽 = 2 𝜇 0 𝑝 / 𝐵 2 means thermal pressure exceeds magnetic pressure locally. Whether magnetic stresses can balance its gradient also depends on the pressure profile, field geometry, and boundary conditions; the ratio alone does not decide force balance.
