12. Introduction to waves in plasmas

A plasma wave is a collective perturbation whose restoring force and inertia are distributed among charged particles and fields. This chapter establishes the common language for the later wave chapters: equilibria, linearization, plane-wave response, dispersion, and controlled limits. The chapter follows the standard progression from small-amplitude wave properties to non-magnetized plasma modes in [12].

12.1 Equilibrium, perturbation, and linearization

How do we turn a nonlinear plasma model into a wave problem? Choose an equilibrium, assign a small parameter to every perturbation, retain terms through first order in that parameter, and then test sinusoidal solutions. The discarded products are precisely where finite-amplitude wave coupling would enter.

Learning objectives

  • state the homogeneous equilibrium and its ordering assumptions
  • linearize continuity, momentum, and Maxwell equations consistently
  • use a plane-wave ansatz to replace derivatives by algebraic operators
  • distinguish longitudinal, transverse, electrostatic, and electromagnetic response
Unit ledger The phase 𝒌⋅𝒓−𝜔𝑡 and normalized variables such as 𝜔/𝜔𝑝,𝑒 are dimensionless.

Polarization describes the direction and time evolution of the electric perturbation. A longitudinal electric field is parallel to 𝒌; a transverse one is perpendicular to it. For a plane wave at nonzero frequency, a purely longitudinal field has no magnetic perturbation by Faraday’s law and is electrostatic in this sense. A transverse electric wave has an accompanying magnetic perturbation even when the equilibrium field is zero.

Acting on the complex plane wave, a time derivative multiplies its amplitude by −𝑖𝜔, and a spatial gradient multiplies it by 𝑖𝒌. These replacements turn the differential equations into algebraic equations for the amplitudes. A dispersion relation specifies which frequencies and wave vectors allow a nonzero solution.

The species equations and Maxwell equations that are linearized are

𝜕 𝑛 𝑠 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝛁 · ( 𝑛 𝑠 𝒖 𝑠 ) = 0 𝑚 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 ( 𝜕 𝒖 𝑠 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝒖 𝑠 ⋅ 𝛁 ( 𝒖 𝑠 ) ) = 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 ( 𝑬 + 𝒖 𝑠 × 𝑩 ) − 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑠 ) 𝛁 × ( 𝑬 ) = − 𝜕 𝑩 𝜕 𝑡 , 𝛁 × ( 𝑩 ) = 𝜇 0 𝒋 + 𝜇 0 𝜀 0 𝜕 𝑬 𝜕 𝑡 𝛁 · ( 𝑬 ) = 𝜌 𝑞 𝜀 0 , 𝛁 · ( 𝑩 ) = 0

where 𝜌𝑞=∑𝑠𝑞𝑠𝑛𝑠 and 𝒋=∑𝑠𝑞𝑠𝑛𝑠𝒖𝑠. For an adiabatic or isothermal closure, the pressure perturbation is written 𝑝𝑠,1=𝛾𝑠𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑠𝑛𝑠,1, with the appropriate temperature ordering stated explicitly. The dimensionless coefficient 𝛾𝑠 specifies the pressure response; 𝛾𝑠=1 gives an isothermal response. It must be chosen for the process being modeled, not inferred from small amplitude alone.

Derivation: linearized Fourier system

Step: Classify the perturbation order

The equilibrium has no flow. Consequently, products of two first-order quantities are second order and do not appear in the linear problem. In particular, the convective term and the perturbed magnetic force are omitted at first order.

Step: Linearize continuity and momentum

The continuity equation becomes

𝜕 𝑛 𝑠 , 1 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝑛 𝑠 , 0 𝛁 · ( 𝒖 𝑠 , 1 ) = 0 .

The first-order momentum equation retains the equilibrium magnetic field but not the product of two perturbations:

𝑚 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 , 0 𝜕 𝒖 𝑠 , 1 𝜕 𝑡 = 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 , 0 ( 𝑬 1 + 𝒖 𝑠 , 1 × 𝑩 0 ) − 𝛁 ( 𝑝 𝑠 , 1 ) .

Step: Apply the plane-wave replacement

For the convention exp(𝑖(𝒌⋅𝒓−𝜔𝑡)), replace derivatives by algebraic operators:

𝜕 𝑓 𝜕 𝑡 → − 𝑖 𝜔 𝑓 , 𝛁 ( 𝑓 ) → 𝑖 𝒌 𝑓 , 𝛁 · ( 𝑨 ) → 𝑖 𝒌 ⋅ 𝑨 , 𝛁 × ( 𝑨 ) → 𝑖 𝒌 × 𝑨 .

Thus continuity and momentum become

− 𝑖 𝜔 𝑛 𝑠 , 1 + 𝑖 𝑛 𝑠 , 0 𝒌 ⋅ 𝒖 𝑠 , 1 = 0

and

− 𝑖 𝜔 𝑚 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 , 0 𝒖 𝑠 , 1 = 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 , 0 ( 𝑬 1 + 𝒖 𝑠 , 1 × 𝑩 0 ) − 𝑖 𝒌 𝑝 𝑠 , 1 .

Step: Form the dispersion condition

Maxwell’s equations receive the same algebraic replacement. Equilibrium neutrality removes the zeroth-order charge density. The remaining linear equations form a coefficient matrix for the field and fluid amplitudes. A nonzero wave exists only when this matrix is singular; its determinant is the dispersion relation.

Knowledge check

  1. Why must the equilibrium be specified before linearizing a plasma model?
    Answer The equilibrium determines which zeroth-order forces cancel and which products are first or second order. Without it, terms such as background flow, background magnetic force, and charge density cannot be classified consistently.
  2. What does the plane-wave ansatz do to a time derivative and a spatial gradient?
    Answer For the convention exp ( 𝑖 ( 𝒌 ⋅ 𝒓 − 𝜔 𝑡 ) ) , 𝜕 𝑓 / 𝜕 𝑡 becomes − 𝑖 𝜔 𝑓 and 𝛁 ( 𝑓 ) becomes 𝑖 𝒌 𝑓 when acting on the amplitude.
  3. What is the difference between longitudinal and transverse polarization?
    Answer Longitudinal fields are parallel to 𝒌 and can create a charge-density perturbation. Transverse fields are perpendicular to 𝒌 and, in the plane-wave Maxwell system, have vanishing divergence of the electric field.
  4. Why is a dispersion relation a determinant condition?
    Answer The linearized equations form a homogeneous matrix equation for the wave amplitudes. A nonzero solution exists only when the matrix is singular, so its determinant must vanish.

12.2 Nonmagnetized response and plasma oscillations

What oscillates when a cold, unmagnetized plasma is displaced? In the high-frequency limit the electrons move against an almost fixed ion background. Charge separation creates an electric restoring force, so the oscillation frequency is set by density and charge, not by a spatial wavelength.

Learning objectives

  • derive the electron plasma frequency from continuity, momentum, and Poisson
  • identify the restoring force in a cold electrostatic oscillation
  • state the correction when both electron and ion inertia are retained
  • separate a localized plasma oscillation from a propagating wave
Unit ledger The plasma frequency 𝜔𝑝,𝑒 is in s−1. The normalized displacement 𝜉/𝜉0 and time 𝜔𝑝,𝑒𝑡 are dimensionless.
Derivation: charge separation supplies the restoring force

Step: Write the electron response

For the electron charge 𝑞𝑒=−𝑒, the cold linearized momentum equation is

− 𝑖 𝜔 𝑚 𝑒 𝒖 𝑒 , 1 = − 𝑒 𝑬 1 .

Longitudinal continuity gives

𝑛 𝑒 , 1 = 𝑛 0 ( 𝒌 ⋅ 𝒖 𝑒 , 1 ) 𝜔 .

For a field parallel to 𝒌, this reduces to

𝑛 𝑒 , 1 = 𝑛 0 𝑘 𝑢 𝑒 , 1 𝜔 .

Step: Close the charge–field loop

The fixed ions do not contribute a perturbed charge. Poisson’s equation is therefore

𝑖 𝑘 𝐸 1 = 𝜌 𝑞 , 1 𝜀 0 = − 𝑒 𝑛 𝑒 , 1 𝜀 0 .

Solving the momentum equation gives

𝑢 𝑒 , 1 = − 𝑖 𝑒 𝐸 1 𝑚 𝑒 𝜔 .

Inserting this response into continuity and then Poisson gives

𝑖 𝑘 𝐸 1 = − 𝑒 𝜀 0 ( − 𝑖 𝑛 0 𝑒 𝑘 𝐸 1 𝑚 𝑒 𝜔 2 ) .

Step: Identify the collective frequency

Cancel the nonzero field amplitude and the common factor 𝑖𝑘:

𝜔 2 = 𝑛 0 𝑒 2 𝜀 0 𝑚 𝑒 .

The wave number disappears because the cold model has no pressure gradient and hence no spatial restoring scale. If the ions also move, repeat the response calculation for every species and add their charge responses. The result is

𝜔 𝑝 2 = ∑ 𝑠 ( 𝑛 𝑠 , 0 𝑞 𝑠 2 𝜀 0 𝑚 𝑠 ) .

For hydrogen, the ion contribution is smaller than the electron term by 𝑚𝑒/𝑚𝑖.

Knowledge check

  1. What provides the restoring force for a cold electron plasma oscillation?
    Answer The electron displacement leaves a small charge imbalance. Poisson’s equation turns that imbalance into an electric field that accelerates the electrons back toward neutrality.
  2. Why does the cold fixed-ion plasma-oscillation frequency not contain the wave number?
    Answer The cold model has no pressure or other spatial restoring term. The charge-separation balance is local, so the frequency is independent of 𝑘 in this limit.
  3. How does allowing the ions to move change the collective frequency?
    Answer Each mobile species contributes 𝑛 𝑠 , 0 𝑞 𝑠 2 / ( 𝜀 0 𝑚 𝑠 ) to the squared collective frequency. The ion contribution is usually small because the ion mass is large.
  4. When does the cold plasma oscillation cease to be an adequate model?
    Answer Finite pressure, collisions, magnetic fields, finite amplitude, boundaries, or kinetic resonances can alter the response. The relevant ordering must be checked before using the cold local result.

12.3 Phase velocity, group velocity, and electromagnetic dispersion

How does a transverse electromagnetic disturbance differ from a local plasma oscillation? The fields must satisfy both Maxwell’s equations and the current response of the electrons. The resulting branch has a cutoff and a wavelength-dependent phase and group velocity.

Learning objectives

  • define phase and group velocity for a one-dimensional branch
  • derive the cold unmagnetized electromagnetic dispersion relation
  • interpret the plasma cutoff and the evanescent regime
  • explain why a phase velocity larger than 𝑐 does not transmit information
Unit ledger The velocities 𝑣phi and 𝑣g are in ms−1. The dielectric factor 𝜀𝑟, 𝑘𝑐/𝜔𝑝,𝑒, and 𝜔/𝜔𝑝,𝑒 are dimensionless.

The relative dielectric factor 𝜀𝑟 summarizes how the induced electron current changes the electromagnetic response; vacuum has 𝜀𝑟=1. It is distinct from the small perturbation parameter 𝜀 used earlier. For a real drive frequency below the plasma frequency, this factor is negative, so 𝑘 is imaginary. A boundary-driven field then decays into the plasma instead of propagating as a bulk wave.

Derivation: current response and the transverse wave equation

Step: Compute the cold current response

The cold electron momentum equation gives

− 𝑖 𝜔 𝑚 𝑒 𝒖 𝑒 , 1 = − 𝑒 𝑬 1 ,

and hence

𝒖 𝑒 , 1 = − 𝑖 𝑒 𝑬 1 𝑚 𝑒 𝜔 .

The perturbed current is

𝒋 1 = − 𝑒 𝑛 0 𝒖 𝑒 , 1 = 𝑖 𝑛 0 𝑒 2 𝑬 1 𝑚 𝑒 𝜔 .

Step: Eliminate the magnetic amplitude

For a transverse plane wave, Faraday’s and Ampere’s equations are

𝒌 × 𝑬 1 = 𝜔 𝑩 1

and

𝒌 × 𝑩 1 = − 𝜔 𝑬 1 𝑐 2 − 𝑖 𝜇 0 𝒋 1 ,

with 𝑐2=1/(𝜇0𝜀0).

Substitute the first relation into the second and use

𝒌 × ( 𝒌 × 𝑬 1 ) = − 𝑘 2 𝑬 1 .

The resulting electric-field equation is

( 𝜔 2 − 𝑐 2 𝑘 2 ) 𝑬 1 = − 𝑖 𝜔 𝒋 1 𝜀 0 .

Step: Read off the electromagnetic branch

Insert the current response and define 𝜔𝑝,𝑒2=𝑛0𝑒2/(𝜀0𝑚𝑒):

( 𝜔 2 − 𝑐 2 𝑘 2 ) 𝑬 1 = 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 2 𝑬 1 .

A nonzero field amplitude therefore requires

𝜔 2 = 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 2 + 𝑐 2 𝑘 2 .

Dividing by 𝜔2 identifies the relative dielectric factor:

𝑘 2 𝑐 2 𝜔 2 = 1 − 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 2 𝜔 2 .

Real 𝑘 requires 𝜔>𝜔𝑝,𝑒. Below the cutoff, 𝑘 is imaginary and the field is evanescent rather than a propagating bulk wave.

Knowledge check

  1. What is the physical meaning of the phase velocity?
    Answer It is 𝑣 phi = 𝜔 / 𝑘 , the speed of a constant phase surface. It need not equal the speed of a localized pulse or of information.
  2. What condition allows the cold electromagnetic branch to propagate in the bulk plasma?
    Answer The wave number must be real, which requires 𝜔 > 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 in the fixed-ion cold model. Below the cutoff the solution is evanescent.
  3. Why does the cold electromagnetic branch approach the vacuum branch at high frequency?
    Answer When 𝜔 ≫ 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 , electron inertia makes the current response small relative to the displacement current. The dielectric factor approaches one and 𝜔 ≈ 𝑐 𝑘 .
  4. Why can the phase velocity exceed the speed of light in this model?
    Answer The phase surface is not a causal signal. For the branch, 𝑣 phi > 𝑐 is accompanied by 𝑣 g < 𝑐 ; signal propagation is governed by the causal packet and medium response.

12.4 Warm-fluid wave equations and kinetic limits

What changes when pressure and particle velocity spread are restored? A warm-fluid closure makes the restoring force depend on wavelength. The resulting branches include warm Langmuir and ion-acoustic limits, while kinetic theory adds resonant particles and phase mixing that no finite set of fluid moments can represent exactly.

Learning objectives

  • derive the warm electrostatic fluid susceptibility
  • identify the warm Langmuir and ion-acoustic limits
  • state the ordering behind the quasi-neutral ion-acoustic approximation
  • explain what kinetic response adds beyond a fluid pressure closure
Unit ledger Thermal speed and ion-acoustic speed are in ms−1, and 𝜆𝐷=𝜀0𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑒/(𝑛0𝑒2) is in m. The phase parameters 𝑘𝜆𝐷 and 𝜔/(𝑘𝑣th,s) are dimensionless.
Derivation: warm-fluid susceptibility and ion sound

Step: Obtain the response of one warm species

For one species in one dimension, Fourier continuity gives

− 𝑖 𝜔 𝑛 𝑠 , 1 + 𝑖 𝑘 𝑛 𝑠 , 0 𝑢 𝑠 , 1 = 0 .

Therefore

𝑛 𝑠 , 1 = 𝑘 𝑛 𝑠 , 0 𝑢 𝑠 , 1 𝜔 .

With the pressure closure, the longitudinal momentum equation is

− 𝑖 𝜔 𝑚 𝑠 𝑢 𝑠 , 1 = 𝑞 𝑠 𝐸 1 − 𝑖 𝑘 𝛾 𝑠 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 , 1 𝑛 𝑠 , 0 .

Define 𝑐𝑠2=𝛾𝑠𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑠/𝑚𝑠. Substitution of continuity and solution for the velocity gives

𝑢 𝑠 , 1 = 𝑖 𝑞 𝑠 𝜔 𝐸 1 𝑚 𝑠 ( 𝜔 2 − 𝑘 2 𝑐 𝑠 2 ) .

The density response is consequently

𝑛 𝑠 , 1 = 𝑖 𝑛 𝑠 , 0 𝑞 𝑠 𝑘 𝐸 1 𝑚 𝑠 ( 𝜔 2 − 𝑘 2 𝑐 𝑠 2 ) .

Step: Sum the species in Poisson’s equation

The longitudinal field obeys

𝑖 𝑘 𝐸 1 = 1 𝜀 0 ∑ 𝑠 𝑞 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 , 1 .

Cancel 𝑖𝑘𝐸1 and insert the species response:

1 = ∑ 𝑠 𝑛 𝑠 , 0 𝑞 𝑠 2 𝜀 0 𝑚 𝑠 ( 𝜔 2 − 𝑘 2 𝑐 𝑠 2 ) .

This is the warm-fluid longitudinal dispersion relation.

Step: Select the two-fluid limits

For a high-frequency electron branch with fixed ions, omit the ion term:

𝜔 2 = 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 2 + 𝑘 2 𝑐 𝑒 2 .

For a low-frequency ion-acoustic branch, neglect electron inertia and impose 𝑛𝑒,1=𝑛𝑖,1 through quasi-neutrality. Electron pressure then supplies the electric field and ion inertia carries the slow wave:

𝜔 2 = 𝑘 2 ( 𝛾 𝑒 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 + 𝛾 𝑖 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑖 ) 𝑚 𝑖 .

This reduction requires 𝜔≪𝜔𝑝,𝑒, 𝑘𝜆𝐷≪1, and |𝜔/(𝑘𝑐𝑒)|≪1. The last condition follows by comparing 𝜔2 with 𝑘2𝑐𝑒2 in the electron susceptibility; it controls neglect of electron inertia relative to pressure. The Debye ordering controls charge separation.

Step: Compare with kinetic response

Kinetic theory replaces the fluid closure by a velocity-space response. For isotropic, non-drifting Maxwellian equilibria of each responding species, with 𝑣th,s=2𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑠/𝑚𝑠, the unmagnetized electrostatic form is

1 + ∑ 𝑠 𝜒 𝑠 ( 𝜔 , 𝑘 ) = 0 ,

where

𝜒 𝑠 = 2 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑠 2 𝑘 2 𝑣 th,s 2 ( 1 + 𝜁 𝑠 𝑍 ( 𝜁 𝑠 ) ) , 𝜁 𝑠 = 𝜔 𝑘 𝑣 th,s .

The analytically continued plasma-dispersion function 𝑍 accounts for resonant particles through causal continuation from ℑ(𝜔)>0. Other distributions require their own velocity-space integral. The imaginary contribution describes resonant energy exchange: the non-drifting Maxwellian response gives Landau damping, while suitable non-equilibrium distributions can drive growth. A warm-fluid 𝛾𝑠 can reproduce selected long-wavelength real-frequency limits, but it cannot reproduce resonant phase mixing.

In the following example, 𝑐𝑠 denotes the collective ion-acoustic speed, as is common in sound-wave notation. It is determined by electron pressure and ion inertia for the stated cold-ion limit, not by the ion pressure-response speed 𝑐𝑖 defined above.

Knowledge check

  1. What new scale enters the warm-fluid electrostatic dispersion relation?
    Answer The pressure response introduces 𝑘 2 𝑐 𝑠 2 , so the frequency depends on wavelength through the species pressure-response speed.
  2. Why can an ion-acoustic wave be low frequency even though the electric field is produced by electrons?
    Answer Electrons establish the pressure-supported electric response quickly, while the large ion inertia carries the slow density motion.
  3. Which ordering justifies the quasi-neutral ion-acoustic limit?
    Answer The mode must be slow compared with the electron plasma response and long compared with the Debye scale: 𝜔 ≪ 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 and 𝑘 𝜆 𝐷 ≪ 1 . Neglecting electron inertia relative to pressure also requires | 𝜔 / ( 𝑘 𝑐 𝑒 ) | ≪ 1 .
  4. What physical effect is absent from a finite warm-fluid closure?
    Answer A finite moment closure does not resolve resonant particles and their phase mixing. Kinetic response is needed for Landau damping or growth and for nonlocal velocity-space structure.

12.5 Reading dispersion diagrams and checking limits

How do we read a dispersion diagram without losing the assumptions? First normalize both axes, then identify real and imaginary wave numbers, slopes, cutoffs, and asymptotic branches.

Learning objectives

  • read cutoffs, slopes, and propagating regions from a dispersion plot
  • use dimensionless variables to compare plasma and vacuum branches
  • check long-wavelength, high-frequency, and weak-plasma limits
  • identify when a branch is evanescent or when a fluid model has left its ordering
Unit ledger The normalized axes use 𝐾=𝑘𝑐/𝜔𝑝,𝑒 and 𝜔̂=𝜔/𝜔𝑝,𝑒, both dimensionless. The reconstructed wave number is in m−1, frequency in s−1, and velocities in ms−1. For warm electrostatic checks, 𝐾𝐷=𝑘𝜆𝐷 is also dimensionless.
Derivation: limiting-case checks

Step: Long-wavelength limit

Start from the normalized transverse relation

𝜔 ̂ 2 = 1 + 𝐾 2 .

As 𝐾→0, 𝜔̂→1. Hence

𝜔 → 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 , 𝑣 g 𝑐 = 𝐾 𝜔 ̂ → 0 .

The phase velocity formally diverges because a nearly spatially uniform oscillation has a finite frequency while its wave number tends to zero.

Step: Short-wavelength and vacuum limits

As 𝐾→∞,

𝜔 ̂ = 1 + 𝐾 2 → 𝐾 .

Therefore

𝑣 phi 𝑐 = 𝜔 ̂ 𝐾 → 1 , 𝑣 g 𝑐 = 𝐾 𝜔 ̂ → 1 .

The plasma becomes transparent and the branch approaches the vacuum line. The same result follows dimensionally when the density tends to zero, because 𝜔𝑝,𝑒→0 and 𝜔=𝑐𝑘.

Step: Below-cutoff behavior

If 𝜔<𝜔𝑝,𝑒, then

𝑘 2 = 𝜔 2 − 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 2 𝑐 2 < 0 .

Write

𝑘 = 𝑖 𝛼 , 𝛼 = 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 2 − 𝜔 2 𝑐 .

Choosing the decaying boundary solution gives the spatial factor exp(−𝛼𝑥). This is an evanescent field.

Step: Compare with the cold electrostatic branch

The cold electrostatic fixed-ion branch has 𝜔=𝜔𝑝,𝑒 for every 𝑘, so its group velocity vanishes. Adding pressure changes the relation to

𝜔 2 = 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 2 + 𝑘 2 𝑐 𝑒 2 .

Pressure therefore supplies spatial propagation.

Knowledge check

  1. What does the intercept of the cold electromagnetic branch represent?
    Answer The intercept 𝜔 ̂ = 1 at 𝐾 = 0 is the plasma cutoff 𝜔 = 𝜔 𝑝 , 𝑒 . Frequencies below it have an imaginary bulk wave number in this model.
  2. How can the slope of a dispersion curve be used physically?
    Answer The local slope d 𝜔 / d 𝑘 is the group velocity for a narrow-band packet. It is distinct from the ratio 𝜔 / 𝑘 , which is the phase velocity.
  3. What is the vacuum limit of the cold electromagnetic branch?
    Answer As density tends to zero or frequency becomes much larger than the plasma frequency, 𝜔 ≈ 𝑐 𝑘 , and both normalized phase and group speeds approach the speed of light.
  4. What is the signature of an evanescent solution in the wave number?
    Answer A frequency below cutoff gives 𝑘 2 < 0 , so one chooses 𝑘 = 𝑖 𝛼 and obtains exponential spatial decay rather than a propagating oscillation.
