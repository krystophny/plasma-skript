5. Single-particle motion

The single-particle model prescribes electromagnetic fields and follows one charged particle through them. It supplies the orbit geometry that guiding-center, kinetic, and fluid descriptions later average or close.

5.1 The Lorentz force and uniform-field gyromotion

What can an electric or magnetic field do to a charged particle? Splitting the Lorentz force into its electric and magnetic parts separates energy change from bending, before any approximation is introduced.

Learning objectives

  • separate the energy-changing and trajectory-bending parts of the force
  • derive the circular orbit in a uniform magnetic field
  • use the charge sign to determine the sense of gyration
𝑚 𝛼 d 𝒗 d 𝑡 = 𝑞 𝛼 ( 𝑬 + 𝒗 × 𝑩 )

𝑞𝛼 and 𝑚𝛼 are the charge and mass of a particle of species 𝛼, for example 𝛼=𝑒 for electrons and 𝛼=𝑖 for ions. Multiplying each field term by 𝑞𝛼 gives its contribution to the force, in N. Dividing the total force by 𝑚𝛼 gives the particle acceleration.

The instantaneous power supplied by the fields is

d 𝑚 𝛼 𝑣 2 2 d 𝑡 = 𝑞 𝛼 𝑬 ⋅ 𝒗

The kinetic energy is in J. The magnetic term does no work because 𝒗⋅(𝒗×𝑩)=0.

In a uniform magnetic field with 𝑬=0, choose 𝑩=𝐵𝒆𝑧. The perpendicular velocity components satisfy

d 𝑣 𝑥 d 𝑡 = 𝜔 𝑐 𝑣 𝑦 , d 𝑣 𝑦 d 𝑡 = − 𝜔 𝑐 𝑣 𝑥 , 𝜔 𝑐 = 𝑞 𝛼 𝐵 𝑚 𝛼

The gyrofrequency 𝜔𝑐 is a signed angular frequency in s−1: positive for ions, negative for electrons. The parallel velocity is constant, so the full orbit is a helix unless 𝑣∥=0.

Step: Balance magnetic bending and inertia

𝑚 𝛼 𝑣 ⟂ 2 𝜌 𝐿 = | 𝑞 𝛼 | 𝑣 ⟂ 𝐵 ,

Step: Decouple the component equations

Differentiating the first component equation once more and inserting the second gives

d 2 𝑣 𝑥 d 𝑡 2 = 𝜔 𝑐 d 𝑣 𝑦 d 𝑡 = − 𝜔 𝑐 2 𝑣 𝑥 ,

Solving the perpendicular component equations gives, for the chosen coordinate orientation,

The guiding center is the center of the circular orbit in the perpendicular plane. Its radius is the Larmor radius or gyroradius 𝜌𝐿=𝑣⟂/|𝜔𝑐|, where |𝜔𝑐| is the positive gyrofrequency. Thus a faster perpendicular particle has a larger orbit, while a stronger field bends the same particle more tightly.

For species 𝛼, write the signed frequency as 𝜔𝑐𝛼=𝑞𝛼𝐵/𝑚𝛼, so that 𝜔𝑐𝑖>0 and 𝜔𝑐𝑒<0; both are angular frequencies in s−1. A thermal orbit estimate uses 𝑣⟂,𝛼=𝑣th,𝛼=2𝑘𝐵𝑇𝛼/𝑚𝛼 in ms−1, giving 𝜌𝐿𝛼=𝑣th,𝛼/|𝜔𝑐𝛼| in m.

Viewed with 𝑩 pointing toward the reader, ions (𝜔𝑐>0) gyrate clockwise and electrons (𝜔𝑐<0) counterclockwise. In both cases the current of the orbiting charge produces a magnetic moment opposite to 𝑩: gyrating particles weaken the applied field, so a plasma is diamagnetic.

5.2 Homogeneous-force drift and the 𝐸×𝐵 drift

A slowly moving orbit center can be found without solving the rapid gyromotion. What velocity makes a constant force balance the magnetic Lorentz force in a uniform field?

Learning objectives

  • derive the 𝐸×𝐵 drift from a gyration-plus-drift ansatz
  • generalize it to the drift velocity for a homogeneous force perpendicular to 𝑩
  • identify which particle properties cancel from the common drift
Unit ledger A drift ratio such as 𝑣𝐷/𝑣⟂ is dimensionless only after both speeds use the same reference state.

Resolve the electric field into components parallel and perpendicular to the magnetic field, with 𝒃=𝑩/𝐵:

𝑬 = 𝐸 ∥ 𝒃 + 𝑬 ⟂ , 𝑚 𝛼 d 𝑣 ∥ d 𝑡 = 𝑞 𝛼 𝐸 ∥

𝐸∥ is the scalar component along 𝑩. It accelerates a particle along the field. The perpendicular component participates in gyromotion and, when static and homogeneous, in the common 𝐸×𝐵 drift derived below.

For uniform, static 𝑬 and 𝑩, try a gyration carried along by a constant drift:

𝒗 = 𝑣 ∥ 𝒃 + 𝒗 ⟂ ( 𝑡 ) + 𝒗 𝐷

All velocities are in ms−1. 𝒗⟂(𝑡) is the gyration of the previous section, 𝑚𝛼d𝒗⟂d𝑡=𝑞𝛼𝒗⟂×𝑩, and 𝒗𝐷 is a constant drift velocity perpendicular to 𝑩.

Insert the ansatz into the Lorentz force. The gyration terms cancel on both sides, d𝒗𝐷/d𝑡=0, and the parallel part was treated above, so the perpendicular remainder is

𝟎 = 𝑬 ⟂ + 𝒗 𝐷 × 𝑩 .

Crossing with 𝑩 and using (𝒗𝐷×𝑩)×𝑩=−𝐵2𝒗𝐷 gives

𝒗 𝐷 = 𝑬 × 𝑩 𝐵 2

With 𝐸 in Vm−1 and 𝐵 in T, 𝐸/𝐵 is directly a speed in ms−1. The common 𝐸×𝐵 drift is independent of species mass and charge sign.

The remainder equation also states what the drift means. In a frame moving with 𝒗𝐷, the perpendicular electric field is 𝑬⟂′=𝑬⟂+𝒗𝐷×𝑩=𝟎, so there the particle only gyrates. In the laboratory the same circle is carried along at 𝒗𝐷. For 𝐸/𝐵≪𝑐 this split is exact, not an average over the orbit.

The same argument holds with 𝑞𝛼𝑬⟂ replaced by any such force. The force balance for a constant orbit-center velocity is

𝑭 + 𝑞 𝛼 ( 𝒗 𝐷 × 𝑩 ) = 0

This is a vector force balance in N. The drift has no acceleration because it describes the constant orbit-center translation.

Crossing with 𝑩 and using 𝒗𝐷⋅𝑩=0 gives

𝒗 𝐷 = 𝑭 × 𝑩 𝑞 𝛼 𝐵 2

The result has velocity units, ms−1. It is valid for a homogeneous perpendicular force and a uniform magnetic field. For 𝑭=𝑞𝛼𝑬 the charge cancels and the 𝐸×𝐵 drift is recovered.

Derivation: the force drift as a cross product

Step: Start from the orbit-center force balance

A constant drift velocity satisfies

𝑭 + 𝑞 𝛼 ( 𝒗 𝐷 × 𝑩 ) = 𝟎 .

The drift is perpendicular to 𝑩, so crossing the equation with 𝑩 uses

( 𝒗 𝐷 × 𝑩 ) × 𝑩 = − 𝒗 𝐷 𝐵 2 .

Step: Solve for the drift

The cross-product equation becomes

𝒗 𝐷 = 𝑭 × 𝑩 𝑞 𝛼 𝐵 2 .

For an electric force, 𝑭=𝑞𝛼𝑬, so both the charge magnitude and its sign cancel. Direct substitution into the original force balance checks the sign of the resulting 𝐸×𝐵 drift.

Gravity, 𝑭=𝑚𝛼𝒈 with the gravitational acceleration 𝒈 in ms−2, does not contain the charge:

𝒗 𝐷 = 𝑚 𝛼 𝑞 𝛼 𝒈 × 𝑩 𝐵 2

Ions and electrons drift in opposite directions, the electrons slower by 𝑚𝑒/𝑚𝑖. In a hydrogen plasma with 𝑛𝑖=𝑛𝑒=𝑛 in m−3 the drifts carry the current density 𝒋=∑𝛼𝑛𝛼𝑞𝛼𝒗𝐷=𝑛(𝑚𝑖+𝑚𝑒)(𝒈×𝑩)/𝐵2 in Am−2.

5.3 Guiding-center approximation and magnetic moment

Real plasmas rarely have perfectly uniform fields. How can a circular orbit remain a useful local picture when the field changes across the orbit? The guiding-center approximation separates the fast gyration from the slow field variation.

Learning objectives

  • state the scale ordering behind guiding-center motion
  • decompose position and velocity into guiding-center and gyromotion parts
  • define the magnetic moment and identify its adiabatic character
Unit ledger Let 𝐿𝐵 denote the magnetic-field variation length in m and |𝜔𝑐|−1 the characteristic gyration time in seconds; the gyroperiod is 2𝜋/|𝜔𝑐|. The ordering parameters 𝜀=𝜌𝐿/𝐿𝐵 and 𝜔slow/|𝜔𝑐| are dimensionless. The magnetic moment defined below is in JT−1, equivalently Am2.

Write the particle position as

𝒓 ( 𝑡 ) = 𝑹 ( 𝑡 ) + 𝝆 ( 𝑡 ) , | 𝝆 | ≈ 𝜌 𝐿

𝑹 is the guiding-center position and 𝝆 is the fast gyration vector. Both positions and 𝜌𝐿 are in m.

The velocity has the corresponding decomposition

𝒗 = d 𝒓 d 𝑡 = d 𝑹 d 𝑡 + d 𝝆 d 𝑡 , d 𝑹 d 𝑡 = 𝑣 ∥ 𝒃 + 𝒗 𝐷 , d 𝝆 d 𝑡 ≈ 𝒗 ⟂

Every velocity is in ms−1. The first equality follows from the position split. The guiding center moves along the field at 𝑣∥ and across it at the slow drift 𝒗𝐷; the fast gyration velocity 𝒗⟂ averages to zero over one turn.

For perpendicular gyromotion, define the magnetic moment

𝜇 = 𝑚 𝛼 𝑣 ⟂ 2 2 𝐵

𝜇 is an energy divided by magnetic field, in JT−1. It is an adiabatic invariant under the stated slow-variation ordering: it is constant to leading order in 𝜀 as the particle samples a slowly changing field. Here “adiabatic” refers to the separation of orbit and field-variation scales, not to thermodynamic heat exchange.

Derivation: magnetic moment, and its invariance from the mirror force

For this energy argument, take a static magnetic configuration with no electric work at the retained order. Keep leading parallel motion along the field when evaluating the sampled change in 𝐵.

Step: Compute the magnetic moment of one orbit

For one circular orbit, the gyroperiod and orbit area are

𝑇 c = 2 𝜋 | 𝜔 𝑐 | , 𝑆 = 𝜋 𝜌 𝐿 2 .

The magnitude of the orbit current is

𝐼 gyro = | 𝑞 𝛼 | 𝑇 c = | 𝑞 𝛼 | | 𝜔 𝑐 | 2 𝜋 .

The magnetic dipole moment is current times area:

𝜇 = 𝐼 gyro 𝑆 = | 𝑞 𝛼 | | 𝜔 𝑐 | 𝜌 𝐿 2 2 = 𝑚 𝛼 𝑣 ⟂ 2 2 𝐵 .

Here 𝜇 is the positive scalar magnitude; the gyration produces a diamagnetic vector dipole.

Step: Use the averaged mirror force

Let 𝑠 measure distance along a field line. The averaged mirror force is

𝐹 ∥ = − 𝜇 𝜕 𝐵 𝜕 𝑠 , 𝑚 𝛼 d 𝑣 ∥ d 𝑡 = − 𝜇 𝜕 𝐵 𝜕 𝑠 .

Since d𝑠/d𝑡=𝑣∥, the particle samples the field according to

d 𝐵 d 𝑡 = 𝑣 ∥ 𝜕 𝐵 𝜕 𝑠 .

Therefore the parallel kinetic energy changes as

d 𝑚 𝛼 𝑣 ∥ 2 2 d 𝑡 = − 𝜇 𝑣 ∥ 𝜕 𝐵 𝜕 𝑠 .

Step: Show adiabatic conservation

The magnetic part changes according to

d 𝜇 𝐵 d 𝑡 = 𝐵 d 𝜇 d 𝑡 + 𝜇 𝑣 ∥ 𝜕 𝐵 𝜕 𝑠 .

Adding the two balances gives

d 𝑚 𝛼 𝑣 ∥ 2 2 + 𝜇 𝐵 d 𝑡 = 𝐵 d 𝜇 d 𝑡 .

In this static, leading-order guiding-center approximation, the total guiding-center energy is conserved, so d𝜇/d𝑡≈0. The ordering requires the field to vary little over one gyroperiod and one gyroradius.

This static mirror argument transfers energy between parallel and perpendicular motion. In a slowly time-varying magnetic field, 𝜇 can remain invariant while an induced electric field supplies energy: the increase of 𝜇𝐵 is then betatron acceleration, and total kinetic energy need not be conserved. Faraday’s law gives this second route directly.

Take a uniform field 𝑩=𝐵(𝑡)𝒆𝑧 that changes slowly, |𝜕𝐵/𝜕𝑡|≪|𝜔𝑐|𝐵, with no parallel electric field and an orbit centred on the symmetry axis, so that the guiding center does not drift.

The induced electric field obeys 𝛁×(𝑬)=−𝜕𝑩/𝜕𝑡. Over one turn it changes the perpendicular energy by

with the line integral taken along the orbit in the direction of motion.

5.4 Nonuniform fields: grad-𝐵, curvature, and mirrors

A nonuniform magnetic field exerts an averaged force on the guiding center. How do field gradients and curved field lines turn the local circular orbit into a drift or a mirror reflection?

Learning objectives

  • derive the grad-𝐵 drift from the magnetic-moment force
  • identify the curvature drift as a response to parallel inertia
  • explain magnetic-mirror reflection using the adiabatic invariant
𝑭 𝜇 = − 𝜇 𝛁 ( 𝐵 )

𝒗 𝐷 𝛁 𝐵 = 𝑭 𝜇 , ⟂ × 𝑩 𝑞 𝛼 𝐵 2 = 𝜇 𝑞 𝛼 𝑩 × 𝛁 ( 𝐵 ) 𝐵 2

5.5 Polarization drift in a slowly varying electric field

The 𝐸×𝐵 drift is an instantaneous force balance. What happens when the perpendicular electric field changes slowly in time? The orbit center acquires an inertial correction so that its drift can follow the changing field.

Learning objectives

  • state the temporal ordering for the polarization-drift approximation
  • separate the static 𝐸×𝐵 drift from the inertial correction
  • derive the polarization drift in a uniform magnetic field
  • interpret the species-summed polarization current
Unit ledger The drive frequency 𝜔d and 𝜔𝑐 are in s−1. The ordering ratio 𝜔d/|𝜔𝑐| is normalized and therefore carries unit [1].

Extend the 𝐸×𝐵 ansatz by a slow inertial correction to the drift:

𝒗 = 𝑣 ∥ 𝒃 + 𝒗 𝐷 + 𝒗 𝐷 pol + 𝒗 ⟂

The leading force balance is the familiar common drift

𝒗 𝐷 = 𝑬 ⟂ × 𝑩 𝐵 2

The leading drift is in ms−1 and follows the instantaneous electric field. As in the guiding-center split, 𝒗⟂ is the rapidly rotating gyration velocity.

The next-order orbit-center response is the polarization drift:

𝒗 𝐷 pol = 𝑚 𝛼 𝑞 𝛼 𝐵 2 𝜕 𝑬 ⟂ 𝜕 𝑡

The factor 1/𝑞𝛼 makes the polarization drift point in opposite directions for positive and negative charges. Unlike the 𝐸×𝐵 drift, it depends on particle mass.

Derivation: inertial correction to the 𝐸×𝐵 drift

Step: Separate the leading drift

Insert the split into the Lorentz force for a uniform magnetic field. As for the 𝐸×𝐵 ansatz, the gyration obeys 𝑚𝛼d𝒗⟂/d𝑡=𝑞𝛼𝒗⟂×𝑩 and cancels, leaving for the perpendicular drifts

𝑚 𝛼 𝜕 𝜕 𝑡 ( 𝒗 𝐷 + 𝒗 𝐷 pol ) = 𝑞 𝛼 ( 𝑬 ⟂ + ( 𝒗 𝐷 + 𝒗 𝐷 pol ) × 𝑩 ) .

The 𝐸×𝐵 drift is defined by

𝑬 ⟂ + 𝒗 𝐷 × 𝑩 = 𝟎 .

Keeping the leading inertial term gives

𝑚 𝛼 𝜕 𝒗 𝐷 𝜕 𝑡 = 𝑞 𝛼 ( 𝒗 𝐷 pol × 𝑩 ) .

Step: Invert the magnetic operator

Cross with 𝑩. Because the correction is perpendicular to the field,

( 𝒗 𝐷 pol × 𝑩 ) × 𝑩 = − 𝐵 2 𝒗 𝐷 pol .

Thus

𝒗 𝐷 pol = 𝑚 𝛼 𝑞 𝛼 𝐵 2 ( 𝑩 × 𝜕 𝒗 𝐷 𝜕 𝑡 ) .

Since 𝑩 is constant,

𝜕 𝒗 𝐷 𝜕 𝑡 = 𝜕 𝑬 ⟂ 𝜕 𝑡 × 𝑩 𝐵 2 .

Use 𝑩×(𝑨×𝑩)=𝐵2𝑨 for 𝑨⋅𝑩=0. The correction is therefore

𝒗 𝐷 pol = 𝑚 𝛼 𝑞 𝛼 𝐵 2 𝜕 𝑬 ⟂ 𝜕 𝑡 .

The discarded term 𝑚𝛼𝜕𝒗𝐷pol/𝜕𝑡 is smaller by 𝜔d/|𝜔𝑐|. The polarization drift is therefore valid when the electric field varies slowly compared with the gyrofrequency.

Summing over species gives a polarization current density

𝒋 pol = ∑ 𝛼 𝑛 𝛼 𝑞 𝛼 𝒗 𝐷 , 𝛼 pol = 1 𝐵 2 ( ∑ 𝛼 𝑛 𝛼 𝑚 𝛼 ) 𝜕 𝑬 ⟂ 𝜕 𝑡

The current density is in Am−2 and 𝑛𝛼 is the density of species 𝛼 in m−3. The charge signs cancel in the species sum, so the mass density weights the polarization current; in an electron–ion plasma the ion contribution is usually larger.

5.6 Cyclotron resonance and circular polarization

The slow polarization approximation has a sharp boundary. How does a rotating electric field exchange energy with a particle when its rotation matches the particle’s gyrofrequency? This matching condition is cyclotron resonance.

Learning objectives

  • represent perpendicular motion with circular complex amplitudes
  • identify the circular polarization that couples to a given charge sign
  • locate the cyclotron-resonance denominator
  • state why an ideal collisionless resonance cannot predict unlimited physical energy
Unit ledger The angular frequency 𝜔, signed gyrofrequency 𝜔𝑐=𝑞𝛼𝐵/𝑚𝛼, and detuning are in s−1. The resonance condition 𝜔/|𝜔𝑐|=1 is normalized and carries unit [1].
𝜕 𝑣 cw 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝑖 𝜔 𝑐 𝑣 cw = ( 𝑞 𝛼 𝑚 𝛼 ) 𝐸 cw

For a harmonic drive 𝐸cw=𝐸̃cwexp(−𝑖𝜔𝑡), the response amplitude is

𝑣 ̃ cw = 𝑞 𝛼 𝐸 ̃ cw 𝑖 𝑚 𝛼 ( 𝜔 𝑐 − 𝜔 ) .

The denominator becomes small when the drive rotation matches the signed gyrofrequency.

Derivation: the resonant circular response

Step: Combine the transverse equations

With 𝑩=𝐵𝒆𝑧, the component equations are

𝜕 𝑣 𝑥 𝜕 𝑡 = ( 𝑞 𝛼 𝑚 𝛼 ) 𝐸 𝑥 + 𝜔 𝑐 𝑣 𝑦 , 𝜕 𝑣 𝑦 𝜕 𝑡 = ( 𝑞 𝛼 𝑚 𝛼 ) 𝐸 𝑦 − 𝜔 𝑐 𝑣 𝑥 .

Define 𝑣cw=𝑣𝑥+𝑖𝑣𝑦 and 𝐸cw=𝐸𝑥+𝑖𝐸𝑦. Adding 𝑖 times the second equation to the first gives

𝜕 𝑣 cw 𝜕 𝑡 + 𝑖 𝜔 𝑐 𝑣 cw = ( 𝑞 𝛼 𝑚 𝛼 ) 𝐸 cw .

Step: Insert a harmonic drive

Use

𝐸 cw ( 𝑡 ) = 𝐸 ̃ cw exp ( − 𝑖 𝜔 𝑡 ) , 𝑣 cw ( 𝑡 ) = 𝑣 ̃ cw exp ( − 𝑖 𝜔 𝑡 ) .

The forced amplitude satisfies

− 𝑖 𝜔 𝑣 ̃ cw + 𝑖 𝜔 𝑐 𝑣 ̃ cw = ( 𝑞 𝛼 𝑚 𝛼 ) 𝐸 ̃ cw ,

so

𝑣 ̃ cw = 𝑞 𝛼 𝐸 ̃ cw 𝑖 𝑚 𝛼 ( 𝜔 𝑐 − 𝜔 ) .

The homogeneous solution is 𝑣cw,0exp(−𝑖𝜔𝑐𝑡). The forced response therefore resonates at 𝜔=𝜔𝑐 for this polarization.

Step: Reverse the circular polarization

With 𝑣ccw=𝑣𝑥−𝑖𝑣𝑦 and 𝐸ccw=𝐸𝑥−𝑖𝐸𝑦,

𝜕 𝑣 ccw 𝜕 𝑡 − 𝑖 𝜔 𝑐 𝑣 ccw = ( 𝑞 𝛼 𝑚 𝛼 ) 𝐸 ccw .

Its denominator is proportional to 𝜔+𝜔𝑐. For positive drive frequency, one circular polarization resonates at 𝜔=|𝜔𝑐|; the matching handedness is determined by the sign of 𝑞𝛼.

A linearly polarized field is the sum of two counter-rotating circular fields. Only the component with the matching handedness resonates, while the other component remains off resonance. In a real plasma, collisions, finite pulse duration, spatial inhomogeneity, and nonlinear effects limit the energy transfer and broaden or shift the ideal resonance.

Knowledge check

  1. Why is circular polarization useful for diagnosing cyclotron resonance?
    Answer It separates a rotating field into the two handednesses. Only the component rotating with the free gyromotion has the small resonant denominator.
  2. How does changing the sign of 𝑞 𝛼 affect the resonant polarization?
    Answer It reverses the signed gyrofrequency and therefore selects the opposite circular handedness for a positive-frequency drive; the positive resonance remains at | 𝜔 𝑐 | .
  3. What does the factor 1 / ( 𝜔 𝑐 − 𝜔 ) predict as the drive approaches resonance?
    Answer The ideal forced-response amplitude grows as the detuning tends to zero. The divergence signals missing broadening or saturation physics, such as collisions, finite pulse duration, or nonlinear motion.
  4. Why can a linearly polarized wave still heat a charged particle at cyclotron resonance?
    Answer A linear polarization is the sum of two circular components. The component with matching handedness supplies the resonant rotating force, while the opposite component is nonresonant.
