16. Plasma sheaths and Langmuir probes

Electrons and ions arrive at a material surface with different thermal speeds, so the surface charges until an electric field filters the particle fluxes. This chapter derives the planar collisionless sheath model, obtains the Bohm entry condition and floating potential, and turns the same flux picture into a Langmuir-probe diagnostic, following the boundary treatment in [12].

16.1 Particle flux and the need for a sheath

A surface collects the particles moving toward it, so the incoming flux is a one-sided velocity moment. At comparable electron and ion temperatures, the electron thermal speed is much larger, and an initially uncharged wall receives a much larger electron current. The resulting negative charging creates the barrier that defines a sheath.

Learning objectives

  • define directed particle flux as a half-space velocity moment
  • derive the isotropic Maxwellian flux to a planar surface
  • compare electron and ion flux scales without confusing flux and density
  • explain why a wall potential must form before steady current balance
Unit ledger The directed particle flux Γ𝑠 is in m−2s−1, so a current density 𝐽𝑠=𝑞𝑠Γ𝑠 is in Am−2. Ratios such as Γ𝑒/Γ𝑖 and 𝑒Φ/(𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑒) are dimensionless.
Derivation: the one-sided Maxwellian flux

Step: Write the isotropic distribution

𝑓 𝑠 ( 𝒗 ) = 𝑛 𝑠 ( 𝑚 𝑠 2 𝜋 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 ) 3 2 exp ( − 𝑚 𝑠 ( 𝑣 𝑥 2 + 𝑣 𝑦 2 + 𝑣 𝑧 2 ) 2 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 ) .

Γ 𝑠 , 0 = ∫ 0 ∞ ∫ − ∞ ∞ ∫ − ∞ ∞ 𝑣 𝑧 𝑓 𝑠 ( 𝒗 ) d 𝑣 𝑥 d 𝑣 𝑦 d 𝑣 𝑧 .

Each tangential Gaussian integral is

∫ − ∞ ∞ exp ( − 𝑚 𝑠 𝑣 𝑥 2 2 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 ) d 𝑣 𝑥 = 2 𝜋 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 𝑚 𝑠 .

Evaluating both tangential integrals leaves

Step: Evaluate the normal half-space integral

Use

∫ 0 ∞ 𝑣 exp ( − 𝑎 𝑣 2 ) d 𝑣 = 1 2 𝑎 , 𝑎 = 𝑚 𝑠 2 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 .

The normal integral is 𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑠/𝑚𝑠, so

Γ 𝑠 , 0 = 𝑛 𝑠 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 2 𝜋 𝑚 𝑠 .

With 𝑣th,s=2𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑠/𝑚𝑠, this becomes

Γ 𝑠 , 0 = 𝑛 𝑠 𝑣 th,s 2 𝜋 .

The mean speed of the same Maxwellian is

𝑣 mean,s = 8 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 𝜋 𝑚 𝑠 ,

Knowledge check

  1. Why is the flux to a plane not 𝑛 𝑠 𝑣 mean,s ?
    Answer Only the velocity component normal to the plane contributes, and only particles with positive normal velocity cross the surface. The angular average and half-space restriction together give Γ 𝑠 , 0 = 𝑛 𝑠 𝑣 mean,s / 4 .
  2. Why can equal electron and ion densities still produce unequal wall currents?
    Answer The flux contains the thermal speed, which scales as 𝑚 𝑠 − 1 / 2 at equal temperature. Electrons therefore arrive much more rapidly than ions even when the volume densities are equal.
  3. What physical process starts the formation of a floating sheath?
    Answer The initial electron flux is larger, so the wall gains electrons and its potential becomes negative relative to the plasma. This potential filters electrons and accelerates ions.
  4. Which assumption makes the half-space flux formula a local estimate rather than a sheath solution?
    Answer The distribution is taken to be isotropic on the plasma side of the boundary. Inside a sheath the electric field makes the distribution anisotropic, so the collected flux must be obtained from the orbit and boundary conditions.

16.2 Sheath structure and the Bohm criterion

The flux argument says that a boundary layer must form, but not how its potential varies in space. A one-dimensional model makes the coupling explicit: Boltzmann electrons respond to the potential, cold ions accelerate through it, and Poisson’s equation determines the curvature. The requirement that a monotone sheath can attach to a quasineutral plasma is the Bohm criterion.

Learning objectives

  • set a consistent wall-to-plasma coordinate and potential reference
  • combine Boltzmann electrons with cold-ion continuity and energy
  • nondimensionalize Poisson’s equation with the electron Debye length
  • derive the sonic lower bound on ion speed at the sheath edge
Unit ledger The coordinate 𝑥 and Debye length 𝜆𝐷 are in m, potential Φ is in V, electric field is in Vm−1, and densities are in m−3. Ion speed and the cold-ion sound speed are in ms−1. The normalized coordinate 𝜉=𝑥/𝜆𝐷, potential barrier 𝜂=−𝑒Φ/(𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑒), and Mach number 𝑀=𝑢𝑠/𝑐𝑠 are dimensionless. The electron Debye length is 𝜆𝐷2=𝜀0𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑒/(𝑛0𝑒2), with 𝜀0 in Fm−1.
Derivation: Boltzmann response, ion flow, and the sheath edge

Step: Write the Boltzmann electron response

For a species in a static electrostatic potential, the stationary Maxwell–Boltzmann response is

𝑛 𝑠 ( Φ ) = 𝑛 𝑠 , ∞ exp ( − 𝑞 𝑠 Φ 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑠 ) .

For electrons 𝑞𝑒=−𝑒 and with Φ(∞)=0 as the plasma reference,

𝑛 𝑒 = 𝑛 0 exp ( 𝑒 Φ 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 ) = 𝑛 0 exp ( − 𝜂 ) .

Step: Use ion continuity and energy

For cold steady ions,

d 𝑛 𝑖 𝑢 𝑖 d 𝑥 = 0 , 𝑛 𝑖 𝑢 𝑖 = 𝑛 𝑠 𝑢 𝑠 = Γ 𝑖 .

The ion momentum equation is

𝑚 𝑖 𝑢 𝑖 d 𝑢 𝑖 d 𝑥 = − 𝑒 d Φ d 𝑥 .

Since 𝑢𝑖d𝑢𝑖/d𝑥=d𝑢𝑖2/2/d𝑥, direct integration gives the conserved energy:

d 𝑚 𝑖 𝑢 𝑖 2 2 + 𝑒 Φ d 𝑥 = 0 .

At the edge, where 𝜂=0,

𝑚 𝑖 𝑢 𝑖 2 2 + 𝑒 Φ = 𝑚 𝑖 𝑢 𝑠 2 2 .

Since 𝑒Φ=−𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑒𝜂,

𝑢 𝑖 2 = 𝑢 𝑠 2 + 2 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 𝜂 𝑚 𝑖 = 𝑐 𝑠 2 ( 𝑀 2 + 2 𝜂 ) .

Continuity then gives

𝑛 𝑖 𝑛 𝑠 = 𝑢 𝑠 𝑢 𝑖 = 𝑀 ( 𝑀 2 + 2 𝜂 ) 1 2 .

Step: Nondimensionalize Poisson’s equation

Gauss’s law reads

𝛁 · ( 𝑬 ) = 𝜌 𝑞 𝜀 0 = 𝑒 ( 𝑛 𝑖 − 𝑛 𝑒 ) 𝜀 0 .

In one dimension, 𝐸𝑥=−dΦ/d𝑥, so

− d 2 Φ d 𝑥 2 = 𝑒 ( 𝑛 𝑖 − 𝑛 𝑒 ) 𝜀 0 .

Set Φ=−(𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑒/𝑒)𝜂 and 𝑥=𝜆𝐷𝜉. The left side becomes

( 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 𝑒 𝜆 𝐷 2 ) d 2 𝜂 d 𝜉 2 .

Using 𝜆𝐷2=𝜀0𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑒/(𝑛0𝑒2) and 𝑛𝑠=𝑛0 at the ideal matching edge gives

d 2 𝜂 d 𝜉 2 = 𝑛 𝑖 𝑛 0 − 𝑛 𝑒 𝑛 0 = 𝑀 ( 𝑀 2 + 2 𝜂 ) 1 2 − exp ( − 𝜂 ) .

Step: Expand at the sheath edge

For small 𝜂,

𝑀 ( 𝑀 2 + 2 𝜂 ) 1 2 = ( 1 + 2 𝜂 𝑀 2 ) − 1 2 ≈ 1 − 𝜂 𝑀 2 ,

while

exp ( − 𝜂 ) ≈ 1 − 𝜂 .

Their difference is (1−𝑀−2)𝜂. If 𝑀<1, the coefficient is negative and the edge curvature has the wrong sign for a monotone positive barrier attached to the plasma. Therefore 𝑀≥1. Equality is the sonic threshold; ion temperature and kinetic effects modify the sound speed and the generalized criterion.

Derivation: electron-free planar edge and Child–Langmuir scaling

Step: Relate ion speed and density to the potential drop

Near a sufficiently negative wall, take the electron density to be negligible and write 𝑉=−Φ>0 for the potential drop from the plasma reference. Let 𝑥 increase from the wall toward the sheath edge, and let the electron-free layer have thickness 𝑑.

The large-drop limit neglects the Bohm entry kinetic energy. Extending this approximation to 𝑉=0 imposes zero injection speed there; this is the idealized Child–Langmuir boundary, not an exact match to the Bohm sheath, whose entry speed is finite. In this idealization ion energy gives

𝑢 𝑖 ≈ 2 𝑒 𝑉 𝑚 𝑖 .

Constant ion flux Γ𝑖=𝑛𝑖𝑢𝑖 then gives

𝑛 𝑖 = Γ 𝑖 𝑚 𝑖 2 𝑒 𝑉 .

Step: Reduce Poisson’s equation

For Φ=−𝑉, Poisson’s equation is

d 2 𝑉 d 𝑥 2 = 𝑒 𝑛 𝑖 𝜀 0 = 𝐶 𝑉 − 1 2 ,

where 𝐶=(Γ𝑖/𝜀0)𝑒𝑚𝑖/2. Introduce the inward distance 𝑠=𝑑−𝑥 measured from the electron-free edge toward the wall. The equation becomes

d 2 𝑉 d 𝑠 2 = 𝐶 𝑉 − 1 2 .

Step: Integrate from the sheath edge

Multiply by d𝑉/d𝑠 and integrate once:

( d 𝑉 d 𝑠 ) 2 2 = 2 𝐶 𝑉 1 2 + 𝐶 1 .

At the idealized injection boundary, 𝑠=0, 𝑉=0, and the separately imposed zero-field condition gives d𝑉/d𝑠=0, so 𝐶1=0. Taking the positive branch gives

𝑉 − 1 4 d 𝑉 d 𝑠 = 2 𝐶 .

Integrating from the edge,

4 3 𝑉 3 4 = 2 𝐶 𝑠 ,

and therefore

𝑉 ( 𝑠 ) = ( 9 𝐶 4 ) 2 3 𝑠 4 3 .

Step: Evaluate the space-charge current

At the wall, 𝑠=𝑑 and 𝑉=𝑉𝑤:

𝑉 𝑤 3 2 = ( 9 𝐶 4 ) 𝑑 2 .

Solving for the ion flux and multiplying by 𝑒 gives the planar Child–Langmuir law:

𝐽 𝑖 = 𝑒 Γ 𝑖 = 4 𝜀 0 9 2 𝑒 𝑚 𝑖 𝑉 𝑤 3 2 𝑑 2 .

This is a near-wall space-charge result. It does not replace the Boltzmann-electron sheath equation across the complete boundary layer.

Knowledge check

  1. Why is the electron density Boltzmann-suppressed in a negative sheath?
    Answer

    For electrons 𝑞𝑒=−𝑒, the equilibrium factor is

    exp ( − 𝑞 𝑒 Φ 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 ) = exp ( 𝑒 Φ 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 ) .

    A negative potential makes this factor smaller than one, so electrons are repelled.

  2. Where does ion acceleration enter the cold sheath model?
    Answer The ion energy integral gives 𝑢 𝑖 2 = 𝑢 𝑠 2 + 2 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 𝜂 / 𝑚 𝑖 . As the potential becomes more negative, eta grows and the ion speed increases; continuity then changes the ion density.
  3. What mathematical sign produces the Bohm criterion?
    Answer Near the quasineutral edge the normalized Poisson equation becomes d 2 𝜂 / d 𝜉 2 ≈ ( 1 − 𝑀 − 2 ) 𝜂 . A monotone positive barrier can attach to the edge only when this coefficient is nonnegative, giving 𝑀 ≥ 1 .
  4. Why is the Child–Langmuir profile not the full sheath solution?
    Answer It neglects electrons and entry kinetic energy, and imposes zero injection speed and field at zero drop. These idealized boundaries produce space-charge scaling; a Bohm sheath instead has finite ion entry speed and matches to a plasma with both species present.

16.3 Floating potential and current balance

A conducting surface can be electrically isolated, externally biased, or held at a prescribed potential. An isolated surface settles at the floating potential, for which the net conventional current vanishes. The mass asymmetry of the incoming flux then enters through one logarithm.

Learning objectives

  • write electron and ion currents with an explicit sign convention
  • derive the exponential electron collection factor for a negative wall
  • obtain the floating potential from zero net current
  • separate the ideal cold-ion result from presheath and surface corrections
Unit ledger The floating potential Φ𝑓 is in V relative to the sheath-edge plasma potential, and 𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑒/𝑒 is the corresponding potential scale. Fluxes are in m−2s−1, current densities in Am−2, and probe area is in m2. The normalized wall bias 𝑢𝑓=𝑒Φ𝑓/(𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑒) and all flux ratios are dimensionless.
Derivation: electron transmission and the floating logarithm

Step: Compute the transmitted electron flux

Let the sheath-edge potential be zero and the wall potential be Φ𝑤<0. An electron has potential energy 𝑞𝑒Φ=−𝑒Φ. It reaches the wall only if its normal kinetic energy exceeds the barrier 𝑒|Φ𝑤|. Integrating the Maxwellian over the transmitted velocities multiplies the unretarded flux by a Boltzmann factor:

Γ 𝑒 ( Φ 𝑤 ) = Γ 𝑒 , 0 exp ( − 𝑒 | Φ 𝑤 | 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 ) = Γ 𝑒 , 0 exp ( 𝑒 Φ 𝑤 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 ) .

The final equality uses Φ𝑤<0.

Step: Write the net current

Take ion current into the wall as positive and electron charge transport as negative:

𝐽 𝑖 = + 𝑒 Γ 𝑖 , 𝐽 𝑒 = − 𝑒 Γ 𝑒 .

Thus

𝐽 ( Φ 𝑤 ) = 𝑒 Γ 𝑖 − 𝑒 Γ 𝑒 , 0 exp ( 𝑒 Φ 𝑤 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 ) .

A floating surface has no external current, so 𝐽(Φ𝑓)=0 and

Γ 𝑒 , 0 exp ( 𝑒 Φ 𝑓 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 ) = Γ 𝑖 .

Taking the logarithm gives

Φ 𝑓 = ( 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 𝑒 ) ln ( Γ 𝑖 Γ 𝑒 , 0 ) .

Step: Insert the ideal edge fluxes

The cold-ion Bohm flux and the unretarded thermal electron flux are

Γ 𝑖 = 𝑛 0 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 𝑚 𝑖 , Γ 𝑒 , 0 = 𝑛 0 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 2 𝜋 𝑚 𝑒 .

Their ratio is 2𝜋𝑚𝑒/𝑚𝑖. Therefore

Φ 𝑓 = ( 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 2 𝑒 ) ln ( 2 𝜋 𝑚 𝑒 𝑚 𝑖 ) .

For hydrogen, 2𝜋𝑚𝑒/𝑚𝑖≈0.00342, whose logarithm is approximately −5.68. The ideal cold-ion floating potential is thus approximately −2.84𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑒/𝑒. The numerical example evaluates these stated definitions without an additional probe-geometry factor.

Knowledge check

  1. Why does the electron current contain an exponential wall-potential factor?
    Answer A negative wall imposes a kinetic-energy barrier on electrons. Integrating the Maxwellian tail above that barrier multiplies the unretarded flux by exp ( 𝑒 Φ 𝑤 / ( 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 ) ) .
  2. What current sign convention is used for a floating surface?
    Answer Conventional current into the surface is positive for ions and negative for electrons: 𝐽 = 𝑒 Γ 𝑖 − 𝑒 Γ 𝑒 . Floating means this signed sum is zero.
  3. Why does the floating potential scale with electron temperature?
    Answer The electron barrier is measured by the dimensionless ratio 𝑒 Φ 𝑓 / ( 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 ) . A hotter electron population requires a larger potential-energy barrier, so the potential drop scales with 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 / 𝑒 .
  4. Why should the coefficient − 2.84 not be transferred unchanged to every experiment?
    Answer It assumes cold ions at the Bohm speed, planar collection, Maxwellian electrons, no secondary emission, and no magnetic or collisional correction. Changing any of these changes the ion-to-electron flux ratio.

16.4 Langmuir-probe current–voltage diagnostics

A Langmuir probe turns the floating-surface balance into a controlled measurement. By sweeping the probe potential relative to the plasma and separating ion, retarding-electron, and electron-saturation regions, one can infer electron temperature, density, and an estimate of the plasma potential. The fit is only meaningful when the collection model and surface physics are stated.

Learning objectives

  • define probe bias and conventional probe current consistently
  • identify ion-saturation, electron-retardation, and electron-saturation regions
  • derive the temperature from the semilog slope of the electron current
  • infer density from the electron saturation scale and list major corrections
Unit ledger Probe bias Φ𝑝−Φpl is in V, current 𝐼𝑝 is in A (often reported in mA), and collection area 𝐴 is in m2. The normalized bias 𝑢=𝑒(Φ𝑝−Φpl)/(𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑒) and normalized current 𝐼𝑝/(𝑒Γ𝑒,0𝐴) are dimensionless. When temperature is quoted in electron-volts, 𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑒 is an energy and 𝑒Φ/(𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑒) is evaluated with the same energy unit.
Derivation: semilog temperature and density inversion

Step: Linearize the retarding electron current

The negative-bias electron flux is

Γ 𝑒 ( Φ 𝑝 ) = Γ 𝑒 , 0 exp ( 𝑒 ( Φ 𝑝 − Φ pl ) 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 ) .

With 𝐼𝑒=−𝑒𝐴Γ𝑒, its magnitude is

| 𝐼 𝑒 | = 𝑒 𝐴 Γ 𝑒 , 0 exp ( 𝑒 ( Φ 𝑝 − Φ pl ) 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 ) .

Divide by the fixed reference 𝐼𝑒,0=𝑒𝐴Γ𝑒,0 and take the natural logarithm to obtain a straight line:

ln ( | 𝐼 𝑒 | 𝐼 𝑒 , 0 ) = 𝑒 ( Φ 𝑝 − Φ pl ) 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 .

Step: Read temperature from the semilog slope

Differentiate with respect to probe potential:

d ln ( | 𝐼 𝑒 | 𝐼 𝑒 , 0 ) d Φ 𝑝 = 𝑒 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 .

Inverting the slope gives

𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 = 𝑒 ( d ln ( | 𝐼 𝑒 | 𝐼 𝑒 , 0 ) d Φ 𝑝 ) − 1 .

If the voltage unit is volts and the energy unit is electron-volts, the numerical value of 𝑘𝐵𝑇𝑒 in eV is the inverse slope in V−1.

Step: Read density from electron saturation

At the extrapolated electron-saturation reference,

| 𝐼 𝑒 , 0 | = 𝑒 𝐴 Γ 𝑒 , 0 .

The Maxwellian half-space flux is

Γ 𝑒 , 0 = 𝑛 𝑒 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 2 𝜋 𝑚 𝑒 .

Solving for density gives

𝑛 𝑒 = | 𝐼 𝑒 , 0 | 𝑒 𝐴 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 2 𝜋 𝑚 𝑒 .

The measured current must be in amperes and the area in square metres for this expression to return 𝑛𝑒 in m−3.

Knowledge check

  1. Which part of an ideal probe characteristic carries the electron-temperature information?
    Answer After subtracting the ion contribution, the slope of ln ( | 𝐼 𝑒 | / 𝐼 𝑒 , 0 ) against probe potential is 𝑒 / ( 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 ) . Fit only the electron-retarding region, excluding the ion and electron saturation regions.
  2. What does the zero-current point represent on a floating probe?
    Answer It is the bias at which the signed ion and electron currents cancel. The point is the probe analogue of the floating wall condition.
  3. Why can the electron saturation current be used to estimate density?
    Answer Under the planar Maxwellian model it is 𝑒 𝐴 𝑛 𝑒 𝑘 𝐵 𝑇 𝑒 / ( 2 𝜋 𝑚 𝑒 ) . Once temperature and collection area are known, the current scale is proportional to density.
  4. Why is an ideal Langmuir-probe fit not automatically a local plasma measurement?
    Answer The probe creates a sheath and samples an orbit-selected collection region. Geometry, magnetic field, collisions, emission, and RF effects can change the current before it is interpreted as a bulk parameter.
